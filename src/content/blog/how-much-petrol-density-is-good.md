The last time I watched a fuel-quality argument at a petrol pump, it was about a number neither of the two men arguing could actually define: *"iska density zyada hai — iska matlab petrol heavy hai."* One claimed denser petrol gives more mileage. The other claimed denser petrol is adulterated. Both were partly right, both were mostly wrong, and neither could tell you what reading is actually *good*.

That question — **how much petrol density is good?** — has a precise, official answer, and knowing it takes about ten minutes. The short version: good petrol sits at **0.740–0.760 kg/l at 15 °C**, the legal range is **0.720–0.775 kg/l**, and outside that band you're either looking at an error, a temperature effect, or something that shouldn't be in the tank. The full reasoning — what density physically is, why "denser = better mileage" is mostly a myth, why E20 quietly disproves it, and how to run the test yourself at any pump — is below. ## What petrol density actually is

Density is simply **mass per unit volume — how many kilograms one litre of the fuel weighs**. Petrol is light: a litre of it weighs roughly three-quarters of a kilogram (0.74–0.76 kg). Water, for comparison, is 1.0 kg a litre; diesel, the heavier sibling, runs 0.82–0.85 kg/l.

It's measured at a fixed reference temperature of **15 °C**, because liquid fuel expands and contracts with heat — a litre of petrol at 40 °C is a slightly different amount of *stuff* than a litre at 15 °C, even though the pump says "1 litre" either way. Every density figure you ever see quoted — the standard, the chart on the pump wall, the hydrometer reading — is meaningless until you know what temperature it was read at.

Why does anyone care? Because **you pay for litres but your engine burns kilograms.** The energy inside the tank is a mass quantity. Density is the bridge between the unit the pump sells you and the unit your pistons actually use.

## The official answer: what density range is good

Indian petrol (BS-VI, IS 2716 specification) must fall within a defined band at 15 °C. Here's how to read any number you're handed:

| Density at 15 °C | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Below 0.720 kg/l | **Too light — outside spec.** Suggests volatile light ends or contamination with naphtha/LPG-type material. Legitimate basis for complaint. |
| 0.720 – 0.740 kg/l | Legal, light end of the range. Usable — but carries slightly less energy per litre. |
| **0.740 – 0.760 kg/l** | **The good band.** Most oil marketing companies target 0.745–0.755 in the blend. This is what "normal" looks like. |
| 0.760 – 0.775 kg/l | Legal, heavy end. Persistent readings up here deserve a second look at the register — but can still be genuine. |
| Above 0.775 kg/l | **Outside spec.** Either heavy components or an adulterant — kerosene (0.78–0.82) and diesel (0.82–0.85) both *raise* density. Complaint warranted. |

For context, diesel's band sits much higher — 0.820–0.845 kg/l — which is one reason you can't compare the two fuels' pump numbers directly, and one reason diesel gives more energy per litre even while its per-kilogram energy content is similar. (The full explanation of why diesel behaves that way is in [why diesel gives more mileage](/blog/why-diesel-gives-more-mileage/).)

**The one-line answer to the title question: 0.740–0.760 kg/l at 15 °C is good. Anything inside 0.720–0.775 is legal. Anything outside both is a problem worth raising at the pump.**

## Why density matters — and how much it actually changes your mileage

The logic behind "denser is better" isn't silly: petrol's energy is around **44 megajoules per kilogram**, so a litre containing more kilograms contains more joules. Run the arithmetic across the legal range:

| Density at 15 °C | Energy in one litre (approx.) | Difference vs. 0.745 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.720 kg/l | ~31.7 MJ | −3.6% |
| 0.745 kg/l (typical) | ~32.8 MJ | baseline |
| 0.775 kg/l | ~34.1 MJ | +4.0% |

So across the *entire* legal range, the energy-per-litre spread is about 7–8% on paper — and noticeably less in practice, because the lighter end of the band is made of smaller molecules that burn slightly more efficiently per kilogram. Two consequences follow:

1. **Within-spec density differences are a small lever, not a big one.** A 2% mileage change from density is real but dwarfed by driving style (20–30%), traffic (20–40%), tyre pressure (2–3%) and speed (10–20%). If someone is choosing a petrol pump based on a 0.005 kg/l difference while flooring it at every green light, the priority order is wrong.

2. **The number's real job is quality control, not mileage optimisation.** A reading inside the band says "this fuel is what it claims to be." That's genuinely valuable — it's the adulteration detector, and it's why the density chart exists on every pump wall.

The honest summary: **good density means legitimate fuel, not miraculous mileage.** If you want the mileage side of the equation, the number that matters is your car's real-world km/l — see [how to calculate petrol cost per km](/blog/how-to-calculate-petrol-cost-per-km/) — and the rupee total comes straight from the [calculator](/calculator).

## The E20 paradox: proof that denser isn't always better

Here's the fact that collapses the "denser = better" myth in one line: **E20 petrol is denser than pure petrol — and it delivers *less* energy per litre.**

Ethanol, the blending agent, has a density of about 0.789 kg/l — heavier than petrol's ~0.745. Blend 20% of it in and the finished fuel's density *rises* to roughly 0.752 kg/l. By the "heavier is better" theory, E20 should improve mileage. It does the opposite: ethanol carries only about 26.8 MJ/kg against petrol's 44, so a litre of E20 holds roughly 5–7% less energy than a litre of pure petrol — showing up in the real world as the 3–5% mileage drop most owners report on engines not optimised for it.

Density went up. Energy went down. The two metrics moved in opposite directions because **density measures mass, and mass is only a proxy for energy when the composition stays the same.** Change the blend and the proxy breaks. This is why the standard defines density as a *quality-compliance* check rather than a performance claim — and why the complete picture of what's in your tank is covered in [what is E20 petrol](/blog/what-is-e20-petrol/) and [how much ethanol is in petrol in India](/blog/how-much-ethanol-in-petrol-in-india/).

## Temperature: the same petrol weighs less on a hot afternoon

Because fuel expands with heat, a litre dispensed at 40 °C contains less mass than a litre at 15 °C — even though both read "1 litre" on the meter. With petrol's expansion coefficient at roughly 0.0009–0.001 per °C:

| Fuel temperature | Density (from a 0.750 baseline) | Energy in a 27.8-litre fill |
|---|---|---|
| 15 °C | 0.750 kg/l | baseline — 20.85 kg |
| 25 °C | 0.743 kg/l | −1.0% — 20.64 kg |
| 35 °C | 0.736 kg/l | −1.9% — 20.45 kg |
| 45 °C | 0.729 kg/l | −2.9% — 20.23 kg |

Two practical readings from that table:

- **A hot-afternoon fill is genuinely lighter than a cool-morning fill.** The difference on a hatchback's ~28-litre tank between filling at 45 °C and 15 °C is about 0.6 kg — roughly ₹55 worth of petrol at today's rates. Real, but small: worth knowing, not worth restructuring your day around. Filling early morning or late evening gets you marginally more energy per rupee.
- **Temperature explains many "bad" readings.** A hydrometer dipped into fuel at 35 °C will show something like 0.736 — which looks alarming next to a 0.745 chart, but is perfectly normal once corrected back to 15 °C. This is why an uncorrected reading proves nothing, and why the correction formula in the testing section below matters.

One caveat worth stating plainly: dispensers are legally verified for *volume* accuracy under India's legal metrology rules, but automatic temperature compensation isn't universally fitted — so the table above reflects what actually arrives in your tank more often than not.

## Density vs octane: two different properties people confuse constantly

Half the density arguments at pumps are really arguments about something else. These are the two properties, separated:

| | **Density** | **Octane (RON)** |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | Mass per litre (kg/l) | Resistance to engine knock |
| Typical petrol value | 0.740–0.760 kg/l | 91 (regular) / 95 (premium) |
| What it affects | Energy per litre; quality compliance | Whether the engine can run its ignition timing safely |
| Who specifies it | IS 2716, checked at every pump | IS 2716, checked at refinery |
| Set by | Overall blend composition | Aromatic/iso-paraffin content, additives |

They're correlated — aromatic molecules are dense *and* high-octane — but they come apart when fuel is adulterated. **Kerosene adulteration raises density and destroys octane:** the fuel looks "heavy" and burns badly, producing knock and deposits. So a high reading isn't a badge of quality; on the wrong side of the band, it's a warning sign. If your engine has started pinging or losing power on "the same petrol," octane — not density — is the property to suspect, and most Indian engines are calibrated for 91 RON: the question of what premium brands like Speed 95 or XP95 actually add is answered in [how petrol is extracted from crude oil](/blog/how-petrol-is-extracted-from-crude-oil/)'s breakdown of fuel grades.

## How to test density at any petrol pump (step by step)

Every petrol pump in India is required to keep the kit: a **calibrated hydrometer** (0.700–0.900 range), a **thermometer**, a measuring jar, and a **density chart** with the day's batch readings in a register. Here's the procedure, which any customer can ask for:

**Step 1** — Ask the attendant for a density check. It's a normal request; refusal itself is informative.

**Step 2** — A sample is drawn from the nozzle into the jar, filled about three-quarters full.

**Step 3** — Dip the hydrometer, let it settle, and read the scale at the bottom of the meniscus. Note the temperature at the same moment.

**Step 4** — Correct to 15 °C. The formula:

**Density at 15 °C = observed reading + 0.0009 × (observed temperature − 15)**

Worked example: you read **0.742 at 30 °C**. Correction: 0.0009 × (30 − 15) = 0.0135. Corrected density = 0.742 + 0.0135 = **0.7555 kg/l** — solidly inside the good band. Without the correction you'd have wrongly judged it light.

**Step 5** — Compare against the chart displayed at the pump *and* the register entry for that batch. The instrument reading and the register should match. A number outside 0.720–0.775, or a mismatch with the register, gives you a legitimate, documented basis for complaint.

For the full version of this test — plus the filter-paper test for adulteration and the 5-litre jar test for quantity short-changing — see [why my motorcycle's mileage dropped: pump tests](/blog/why-my-motorcycle-mileage-dropped-fuel-pump-tests/), where I worked through all three against real pumps.

## So: should density decide where you fill up?

No — but *trust* should. Here's the honest priority list, ordered by how much each factor moves your actual fuel bill:

1. **Pump integrity** (meter accuracy, no adulteration) — worth 2–10%, occasionally more. A reputable pump with a clean register beats a clever-sounding density number every time.
2. **Your real-world mileage** — worth 20–40% between a smooth and a heavy foot. It's the input that dominates [cost per km](/blog/how-to-calculate-petrol-cost-per-km/), not density.
3. **City price differences** — worth ₹10–15 a litre across states, which is ₹300+ on a 500 km trip. Check [today's petrol price by city](/petrol-price-in-india/) before any intercity fill-up.
4. **Density within the legal band** — worth about 2%, and only in the direction of "is this fuel legitimate."
5. **Temperature timing** — worth ~₹50 a tank. Nice, not necessary.

Density is a *pass/fail quality check*, not a shopping criterion. If it's inside 0.720–0.775 and matches the register, the pump is fine — go and spend the attention you saved on something with a bigger lever, like tyre pressure or your next [fill-up log entry](/blog/how-to-calculate-petrol-cost-per-km/).

## Common myths about petrol density

- **"Denser petrol always gives more mileage."** Within the legal band, the energy spread is ~2% in practice — swamped by driving, traffic and speed. And E20 is denser yet less energetic, which breaks the rule outright.
- **"Foam in the nozzle means less petrol / bad density."** Foaming is an aeration and metering phenomenon affected by tank levels and nozzle speed — not a density measurement. The quantity check is the 5-litre jar test, not your eyes.
- **"A low reading means the pump is diluting the fuel."** Often it's just temperature. Uncorrected readings taken on a hot afternoon routinely look 1–2% low. Always correct to 15 °C before judging.
- **"Higher density means higher octane."** Different properties. Adulteration can push density up while octane collapses — the engine notices the octane loss long before you notice the density gain.
- **"My pump showed 0.760, so it's better fuel than the pump showing 0.745."** Both are inside spec. The difference is a rounding error in your tank and a distraction from the real variables.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good petrol density in kg/l?

**0.740–0.760 kg/l at 15 °C is the good band**, with most Indian oil companies blending to around 0.745–0.755. The full legal range for BS-VI petrol is 0.720–0.775 kg/l at 15 °C. Readings below 0.720 or above 0.775 are outside specification and justify a complaint at the pump.

### Does higher density mean better mileage?

Only weakly, and only within the legal range: the energy-per-litre difference across the entire spec band is about 2% in real-world terms — far less than driving style, traffic or speed, each of which swings mileage by 10–40%. Density is primarily a fuel-quality indicator. Also, E20 petrol is denser than pure petrol yet delivers less energy per litre, which disproves the "denser is always better" rule directly.

### How can I check petrol density myself?

Ask the attendant for a density test — pumps are required to carry a hydrometer, thermometer and density chart. Take the reading, note the fuel temperature, and correct it: density at 15 °C = reading + 0.0009 × (temperature − 15). For example, 0.742 at 30 °C corrects to 0.7555 kg/l — inside the good band. Compare with the pump's register; a mismatch or an out-of-range number is grounds for complaint.

### Does petrol density change with temperature?

Yes — petrol expands as it heats, so density falls about 0.09% per °C. A reading of 0.750 at 15 °C becomes roughly 0.736 at 35 °C and 0.729 at 45 °C. This means a hot-afternoon fill contains about 2–3% less fuel *mass* than a cool-morning fill of the same volume — around 0.6 kg, or ₹50–55 worth, on a hatchback's tank. It also means uncorrected hydrometer readings are unreliable.

### Does E20 change petrol density?

Slightly upward. Ethanol's density (~0.789 kg/l) is higher than petrol's (~0.745), so 20% blending lifts finished-fuel density to roughly 0.752 kg/l — still comfortably inside the legal 0.720–0.775 band. A normal E20 reading is not adulteration. But despite being denser, E20 holds less energy per litre, which is why it can cost you 3–5% of your mileage on engines not optimised for it — details in [what is E20 petrol](/blog/what-is-e20-petrol/).

### Can density tell me if petrol has been adulterated?

It's one of the better field tests. Kerosene (0.78–0.82 kg/l) and diesel (0.82–0.85 kg/l) adulteration both push density above 0.775; lighter contaminants push it below 0.720. A reading outside spec — especially one that disagrees with the pump's own register — is a legitimate basis for complaint. For a fuller testing routine (filter paper, density, quantity jar), see the [pump testing guide](/blog/why-my-motorcycle-mileage-dropped-fuel-pump-tests/).

### Is denser petrol better for bikes and cars?

Inside the legal range, the difference is negligible for any vehicle — a couple of percent of energy per litre, overwhelmed by how you ride or drive. What matters far more is using the correct octane grade for your engine, fuel from a trusted pump, and keeping your own real-world mileage measured. Bikes are more sensitive to *octane* and contamination than to within-spec density variation.

### Why do diesel and petrol have different densities?

Different molecules. Diesel's longer-chain hydrocarbons pack more mass into a litre (0.820–0.845 kg/l) than petrol's lighter ones (0.720–0.775 kg/l). That's also why diesel carries more energy per litre — the reason diesel cars return better mileage per unit of volume even though energy per *kilogram* is close between the two fuels. The full mechanism is in [why diesel gives more mileage](/blog/why-diesel-gives-more-mileage/).

### Does buying denser petrol save money?

Not meaningfully, no — provided the fuel is inside spec (which is the part worth verifying). At today's prices, the entire legal density range is worth about 2% of your fuel cost, while your driving behaviour is worth 20–40% and city price differences are worth ₹300+ per trip. ## Final thoughts

Petrol density has a clean, official answer: **0.740–0.760 kg/l at 15 °C is good, 0.720–0.775 kg/l is legal, and outside that something is wrong** — a temperature effect you didn't correct for, or fuel that isn't what it claims to be. Within the legal band, though, density is a quality certificate rather than a performance upgrade: the mileage difference it can buy you is around 2%, which is smaller than almost every other variable in your tank.
