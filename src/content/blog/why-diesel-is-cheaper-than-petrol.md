My neighbour drives a diesel hatchback and is quite proud of it. Last month we ended up at the same pump on the same evening — he filled diesel, I filled petrol — and he looked at the display with deep satisfaction. "See? Diesel is always cheaper. It's subsidised, and it's cheaper to refine. Government looks after the common man."

I didn't argue at the pump. But his answer bothered me on the drive home, because neither half of it is quite right, and the real answer is far more interesting than "subsidy." Diesel in India is cheaper because of a set of deliberate policy choices — lower central excise, lighter state VAT in most states, and a pricing history that goes back to when diesel was literally kept artificially cheap for decades. The refining story everyone repeats turns out to be a minor footnote.

I went digging through PPAC price build-ups, excise notifications, and a Lok Sabha answer that actually published the full component-wise build-up for Delhi, and worked the numbers out properly. Here's the whole thing — including the part where I found that in August 2026, diesel's raw cost before any tax was actually *higher* than petrol's, and taxes were doing all the work.

## The short answer

Diesel is cheaper than petrol in India for three reasons, in this order of importance:

**1. Lower central excise duty.** The centre charges ₹11.90 per litre of petrol and ₹7.80 per litre of diesel — a ₹4.10 gap baked in at the national level.

**2. Lower state VAT in most states.** States tax diesel lighter than petrol, usually by 2–5 percentage points, because diesel is the fuel of trucks, buses, and tractors — and taxing it hard pushes up the price of literally everything else.

**3. History.** Diesel was kept administratively cheap for decades, and was only fully deregulated in October 2014. Petrol was freed back in 2010.

There is no fuel subsidy involved. Since deregulation, the government doesn't pay the oil companies anything to keep diesel's price down — diesel just sits in a lighter tax bracket. And the refining argument, while not entirely wrong, is a small part of the story at best. The rest of this post shows exactly why, with real numbers.

## The official build-up: where every rupee of the gap comes from

Rather than hand-wave, let's use an actual official source. In August 2026, a price build-up of petrol and diesel in Delhi was laid out in Parliament, sourced from PPAC. It looks like this:

| Component (per litre, Delhi) | Petrol | Diesel |
|---|---|---|
| Price charged to dealers (incl. central excise, excl. VAT) | ₹81.08 | ₹78.25 |
| Dealer commission | ₹4.45 | ₹3.04 |
| State VAT (incl. VAT on dealer commission) | ₹16.59 | ₹13.91 |
| **Retail selling price** | **₹102.12** | **₹95.20** |

So in Delhi, diesel was ₹6.92 cheaper than petrol per litre. Now the interesting part — where does that ₹6.92 actually come from? Let's split the first row, because it hides the central excise duty:

- Petrol: ₹81.08 − ₹11.90 excise = **₹69.18 pre-tax fuel cost**
- Diesel: ₹78.25 − ₹7.80 excise = **₹70.45 pre-tax fuel cost**

Read that again: in August 2026, the raw cost of a litre of diesel (crude, refining, freight, marketing margin) was about ₹1.27 *higher* than petrol's. Diesel's entire discount at the pump was manufactured by everything stacked on top of it:

| Gap component | Advantage to diesel |
|---|---|
| Central excise duty (₹11.90 vs ₹7.80) | ₹4.10 |
| Dealer commission (₹4.45 vs ₹3.04) | ₹1.41 |
| State VAT (₹16.59 vs ₹13.91) | ₹2.68 |
| Pre-tax fuel cost (₹69.18 vs ₹70.45) | −₹1.27 |
| **Net gap at the pump** | **₹6.92 cheaper** |

That's the whole answer in one table. Three layers of tax and commission hand diesel an ₹8.19 advantage, its slightly higher raw cost claws ₹1.27 back, and you get the ₹6.92 you see on the display.

If you want the general machinery behind how those layers are built — why VAT is calculated on a subtotal that already contains excise duty, and why that compounding matters — I worked through that step by step in [how fuel excise duty and state VAT are actually calculated](/blog/how-fuel-excise-duty-and-state-vat-are-calculated/). What follows here is what's *different* about diesel specifically.

## Reason 1: the centre charges diesel less excise duty

Central excise on fuel is a flat per-litre amount — same in every state — and it has always been set differently for petrol and diesel. Today's structure, after the March 2026 cut:

**Petrol — ₹11.90/litre:** ₹1.40 basic excise + ₹3.00 special additional excise + ₹2.50 agriculture infrastructure cess + ₹5.00 road and infrastructure cess.

**Diesel — ₹7.80/litre:** ₹1.80 basic excise + ₹0 special additional excise + ₹4.00 agriculture infrastructure cess + ₹2.00 road and infrastructure cess.

Notice diesel's basic excise (₹1.80) is actually *higher* than petrol's (₹1.40). The gap comes almost entirely from the special additional excise duty — ₹3.00 on petrol, zero on diesel — and the road cess, where petrol pays ₹5.00 and diesel ₹2.00. The cesses carry names like "agriculture infrastructure" for a reason: the ₹4.00 diesel pays there is essentially diesel money being earmarked back for farms — the same sector that the lower diesel price is meant to protect.

The ₹4.10 gap itself has been fairly stable, but the absolute levels have moved around a lot. This is the recent history:

| Effective date | Petrol excise | Diesel excise | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 May 2020 | ₹32.98 | ₹31.83 | ₹1.15 |
| 2 November 2021 | ₹27.90 | ₹21.80 | ₹6.10 |
| 8 April 2025 | ₹19.90 | ₹15.80 | ₹4.10 |
| 1 November 2025 | ₹21.90 | ₹17.80 | ₹4.10 |
| 27 March 2026 | ₹11.90 | ₹7.80 | ₹4.10 |

Two things stand out. First, in May 2020 the excise on the two fuels was nearly identical — ₹1.15 apart — which means tax policy alone wasn't producing much of a gap at that moment. Second, the November 2021 cut deliberately slashed ₹10 off diesel and only ₹5 off petrol, and *that* is when the centre really engineered today's differential. The April 2025 revision (₹8 off petrol, ₹6 off diesel) trimmed it back to ₹4.10, where it has stayed through the November 2025 increase and the March 2026 West Asia crisis cut.

About that March 2026 cut: crude spiked from around $70 to $122 a barrel in under four weeks, the government chopped ₹10 per litre of excise off *both* fuels — and pump prices didn't move at all. The cut was deliberately not passed on to us; it went to cushion the oil marketing companies' losses while retail prices stayed frozen. Which is a good reminder that excise changes and pump-price changes are two different events, a distinction I explained further in [why petrol prices change at 6 AM every day](/blog/why-petrol-prices-change-at-6am-and-how-omcs-calculate-them/).

## Reason 2: states deliberately tax diesel lighter

The bigger variable — and the one that makes the gap ₹4 in one state and ₹10 in another — is state VAT. Since VAT is charged as a percentage on (fuel cost + excise + dealer commission), even a small rate difference gets amplified, and it compounds the central gap on top of itself.

State VAT rates on petrol vs diesel, per Indian Oil's published figures:

- **Delhi:** about 19.4% on petrol vs 17.1% on diesel (back-calculated from the official build-up above)
- **Mumbai:** 25% on petrol, 21% on diesel
- **Kolkata:** 25% on petrol, 17% on diesel
- **Chennai:** 13% on petrol, 11% on diesel

Same pattern everywhere: diesel gets the lower rate. The reason is political economy, not chemistry.

**Diesel is the economy's fuel.** It powers the trucks that move food across states, the buses that carry workers, the pumps that irrigate farmland, the gensets that keep factories running. Raise diesel's tax and you don't just hurt the driver — within weeks it shows up in the price of vegetables, cement, and bus tickets. Governments of every party have learned that lesson the expensive way, so diesel stays in the gentle tax bracket.

**Petrol is the household's fuel, and a better revenue subject.** Petrol demand comes disproportionately from private two-wheelers and cars — people who are, on average, more able to pay, and who can't easily pass the cost on to someone else. States raise more from taxing it without triggering the inflation knock-on effects.

**Farm politics.** Diesel is the single biggest input cost for farmers after seed and fertiliser. A heavy diesel tax in an agricultural state is an electoral statement. The Centre's own ₹4 agriculture cess on diesel, and its exemption structures around agricultural use, show the same instinct.

The state-level spread is exactly why the gap looks so different around the country — and why sometimes it nearly vanishes. In Delhi in late June 2020, after the city raised its VAT on diesel sharply, a litre of diesel came within about a rupee of petrol — for a few weeks, the two nozzles were almost at parity. It's also why people near state borders play the [cross-border filling game](/blog/why-petrol-costs-more-one-state-over-border-hopping-trick/) — except the relevant gap for them is between two states' diesel rates, not petrol's. Our [state-wise price ranking](/blog/state-wise-petrol-price-ranking-2026/) shows which states tax hardest overall; diesel follows the same ordering, just a few rupees lower.

## Reason 3: diesel was actually kept artificially cheap for decades

The "subsidy" idea my neighbour had isn't invented from nothing — it's a memory of how India used to price diesel.

Until 2010, both petrol and diesel prices were set by the government, not the market. Oil marketing companies were forced to sell diesel below their cost of supply, and the gap — called "under-recovery" — was patched together from the government, upstream oil companies, and the profits OMCs made on petrol. Petrol was freed first, in June 2010, on the Parikh Committee's recommendation. Diesel stayed under admin control for four more years, precisely because every government feared what touching its price would do to inflation. Diesel was fully deregulated only in October 2014, and moved to daily price revision along with petrol in June 2017.

So for most of India's motoring history, diesel's discount wasn't a tax choice — it was a real, budgeted price gap held in place by administrative order. Two consequences outlived the policy:

1. **The expectations stuck.** A generation of drivers, fleet operators, and even car dealers grew up knowing "diesel is cheaper," and that instinct still shapes buying decisions today — something [which car is best, petrol or diesel](/blog/which-car-is-best-petrol-or-diesel/), examines against 2026's actual numbers.

2. **The mechanism changed, but the result didn't.** After 2014, the subsidy disappeared entirely — there is no line in the budget keeping diesel cheap any more — but the centre and states simply kept taxing it more lightly, reproducing the same outcome through the tax code instead of a subsidy.

Even today's market isn't entirely market-like. Through 2026's West Asia crisis, pump prices have been frozen while crude stayed far above where the build-up would put them — the same under-recovery mechanics the 2010s were supposed to end, temporarily back in service. When you next see a petrol-diesel gap, remember it's a policy artefact in more ways than one.

## What about refining? The part everyone gets slightly backwards

The standard explanation is "diesel is cheaper to refine, so it's cheaper." Half right, and at today's numbers, mostly irrelevant.

**The true half:** a barrel of crude naturally yields more diesel than petrol. A typical Indian refinery configured for Indian demand produces roughly twice as much middle distillate (diesel, jet fuel) as petrol from the same barrel, and petrol's share often has to be *manufactured* by cracking heavier molecules in conversion units — real equipment, real energy, real cost. For decades, Indian refineries were in fact built diesel-first, because India's demand was diesel-first.

**The irrelevant half:** look back at the official build-up. In August 2026, diesel's pre-tax cost was ₹70.45 a litre against petrol's ₹69.18 — diesel was the *more* expensive fuel to put into the tank before anyone added a rupee of tax. Why? Two reasons. India's refining cost advantage on diesel is smaller than it used to be — the 2020 shift to BS-VI fuel meant far deeper sulphur removal for both fuels, hitting diesel's hydrodesulphurisation harder. And refinery-gate costs don't move in a vacuum: through 2026's supply disruption, international diesel (gasoil) prices spiked harder than petrol, because diesel is the world's commercial fuel and supply shocks hit it first.

So refining explains why diesel isn't *more* expensive than petrol — it doesn't explain why it's ₹7 cheaper. That's taxes, as the component table showed.

## The part that isn't about price at all

Here's the twist that makes per-litre comparisons misleading: a litre of diesel simply contains more usable energy than a litre of petrol — roughly 38.6 MJ against 34.2 MJ, about 13% more — and a diesel engine converts a larger share of that energy into motion, around 40–45% thermal efficiency in a modern diesel versus roughly 25–30% in a petrol engine. Both effects, and the exact conditions where the advantage shrinks, are worked out in [why diesel gives more mileage than petrol](/blog/why-diesel-gives-more-mileage/).

Put together, that's why a diesel car typically returns 20–30% more kilometres per litre than an equivalent petrol one, and why diesel's *per-kilometre* cost advantage has historically been bigger than the ₹6–7 per-litre gap suggests. For what that's actually worth in real tracked numbers — maintenance, upfront price gap, breakeven point — see [petrol vs diesel vs CNG running costs](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/). The short version: the cheaper litre helps diesel, but the extra kilometres are what made diesel unbeable for a decade.

## Why the gap is nothing like it used to be

If you bought fuel in the mid-2010s, you remember diesel being ₹20–25 cheaper than petrol — sometimes more. Today it's ₹6.92 in Delhi, and single digits almost everywhere. What changed?

- **Deregulation (2014)** removed the artificial discount and let diesel's base price find its level.
- **The 2020–21 excise hikes** pushed both fuels up hard; because VAT compounds on top of excise, the hikes hit petrol's higher rate harder in absolute rupees.
- **The November 2021 cut** (−₹10 diesel, −₹5 petrol) re-engineered the gap to its current shape.
- **States quietly raised diesel VAT too** through the 2010s — Delhi's diesel tax today would have been unthinkable in 2013.

There's no law of nature holding the gap at ₹7. It's entirely a function of decisions that get revisited — every union budget, every state budget, every crisis. The gap could widen again (a diesel-friendly cut) or narrow to near zero in high-VAT states, and a couple of states have already seen diesel touch or cross petrol's price at times.

## Can the gap close completely — or even flip?

Yes, and in places it has. Two scenarios to watch:

**A state raises diesel VAT to petrol levels.** This is what Delhi nearly did in 2020. Because diesel's raw cost is currently running *above* petrol's (crude and refining being what they are in 2026), equalise the taxes and diesel would actually end up more expensive per litre.

**GST.** If petrol and diesel ever came under GST, there would be one national rate for both and no state-level differential at all — the entire state-VAT layer of the gap would vanish overnight. Every finance minister since 2017 has been asked about this; none has done it, because state VAT on fuel is one of the biggest revenue lines state governments have. Until the states agree to give that up, the differential stays.

## How to check the gap in your own city

**Step 1** — Pull today's prices for your city from our [diesel price hub](/diesel-price-in-india/) or your OMC's app (Fuel@IOC, HP Pay, BPCL SmartDrive).

**Step 2** — Get the component build-up from PPAC (ppac.gov.in) or the price-breakup pages your oil marketing company publishes — you want the four layers, not just the final number, for both fuels.

**Step 3** — Subtract: (petrol excise + VAT + commission) vs (diesel excise + VAT + commission). That difference is the policy gap; whatever's left over is the raw fuel-cost difference.

**Step 4** — Check your state's diesel VAT percentage against a neighbouring state's. Remember it applies to a subtotal that already includes excise — that compounding is explained in the [excise and VAT walkthrough](/blog/how-fuel-excise-duty-and-state-vat-are-calculated/).

**Step 5** — Re-check after any budget. Both the centre and states revise these rates a few times a year, and crisis-driven moves (like March 2026's ₹10 cut) can land with no warning at all.

## Common mistakes people make

- **"Diesel is subsidised."** Not since October 2014. It's taxed lighter — which looks identical at the pump but is a completely different mechanism with a completely different budget story.
- **"It's cheaper because refining costs less."** The official build-up says diesel's pre-tax cost was *higher* than petrol's in August 2026. Refining shapes the floor; taxes build the gap.
- **"The gap is about ₹7 everywhere."** It's ₹6.92 in Delhi and different in every state — VAT rates alone span a wide range, and percentage VAT means the gap grows with the base price too.
- **"Cheaper per litre means cheaper to run."** Per-litre and per-kilometre are different comparisons. Diesel's extra energy content and efficiency matter more than the pump gap — and the upfront price, maintenance, and resale math can swamp both, as [which car is best, petrol or diesel](/blog/which-car-is-best-petrol-or-diesel/) works out.
- **"Diesel will always be cheaper."** The gap is a policy artefact that has already shrunk from ₹25 to ₹7 in a decade. Equalise VAT, or bring fuel under GST, and the arithmetic changes overnight.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is diesel cheaper than petrol because of a government subsidy?

No. Diesel pricing was deregulated in October 2014 and there has been no fuel subsidy on it since. Diesel is cheaper because the centre charges about ₹4 less excise duty per litre and most states apply a lower VAT percentage — a lighter tax bracket, not a subsidy.

### Why is diesel cheaper than petrol when crude oil is the same?

Both fuels start from the same barrel of crude, but they don't end at the same pre-tax price (in 2026 diesel's pre-tax cost has actually been slightly higher), and they're taxed differently. Central excise differs by ₹4.10 a litre, dealer commission by about ₹1.40, and state VAT by 2–5 percentage points — together worth around ₹8 a litre, which is where the ₹6–7 you see at the pump comes from.

### Will diesel always stay cheaper than petrol in India?

Nothing structural guarantees it. The current gap is set by excise rates and state VAT percentages, both of which are revised regularly — diesel was within a rupee of petrol in Delhi in mid-2020, and the gap was over ₹20 in the mid-2010s. GST inclusion or a state-level VAT change could shrink it to near zero.

### Is diesel actually cheaper to refine than petrol?

Somewhat — a barrel of crude yields more diesel than petrol, and petrol often needs extra conversion processing. But it's a small factor at the pump: in the official August 2026 Delhi build-up, diesel's cost before tax was about ₹1.27 per litre *higher* than petrol's. Refining explains why diesel isn't far more expensive, not why it's cheaper.

### Why has the petrol-diesel gap shrunk from ₹25 to under ₹10?

Deregulation in 2014 removed the artificial discount, the 2020–21 excise hikes and later revisions locked the central differential at ₹4–6 per litre, and states raised their diesel VAT over the same period. The mid-2010s gap was a leftover of four decades of administered pricing; today's is a deliberate, maintained tax differential.

## Final thoughts

The ₹6–7 diesel has on petrol is not a gift, not a subsidy, and not really a refining discount — it's a tax decision, visible rupee by rupee in the official build-up: ₹4.10 from the centre, ₹1.41 from dealer commission, ₹2.68 from the state, minus ₹1.27 that diesel's slightly higher raw cost takes back. Every single one of those numbers is set by someone, and someone can change it.

So next time the pump attendant switches on the diesel nozzle and the display starts ₹7 behind petrol's, you'll know exactly why — and you'll also know it's a "why" with an expiry date printed on every union and state budget.
