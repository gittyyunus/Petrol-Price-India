My Activa used to give me a steady 45-ish km/l. Then one month it dropped to about 36, and stayed there for almost three weeks. I changed nothing. Same route, same riding style, same tyre pressure — I'd actually started checking that after writing about mileage habits earlier. I got the carburetor checked, the mechanic found nothing wrong, and I was genuinely stumped.

Then a colleague mentioned, almost casually, that a pump near my new office had been in the local news for a density complaint a few months back. I switched back to my old pump on the other side of town for two weeks. Mileage went right back to 44-45.

I'm not going to pretend I "proved" adulteration with a lab report or anything. But it was enough of a pattern that I started actually learning the tests you're legally allowed to ask for at any pump in India. Most people don't know these rights exist. I didn't either, until that Activa forced me to find out.

## Two different problems, and they're not the same thing

Before jumping into tests, it's worth separating two issues that get lumped together but are actually different:

**Adulteration** — mixing petrol or diesel with cheaper substances (kerosene is the classic one, sometimes solvents) to increase volume and profit margin at the pump owner's cost, while quietly damaging your engine over time.

**Short measurement** — you're getting genuine, unadulterated fuel, just less of it than what the meter says and what you paid for. This is a mechanical/electronic tampering issue with the dispensing unit, not a fuel quality issue.

Both cost you money. Only one damages your engine. And the tests for catching them are different, so it helps to know which one you're actually checking for.

## Test 1: The filter paper test (for adulteration)

This is the one most people have vaguely heard of but never actually asked for. Here's exactly how it works, and every retail outlet in India is required to keep filter paper on hand for this.

**Step 1** — Ask the attendant for a filter paper test before or after filling up. It's your right, and it's free.

**Step 2** — The nozzle gets wiped clean, then a couple of drops of the fuel are placed on a plain white filter paper (or the attendant does this directly from the nozzle).

**Step 3** — Wait about two minutes.

**Step 4** — Pure petrol evaporates completely, leaving no mark. A yellowish, brownish, or oily stain left behind suggests adulteration.

One thing that confused me the first time I read about this: petrol itself has a faint pinkish tint (from the dye added to it), so a light pink tinge on the paper is normal and not a stain — don't mistake the fuel's own colour for a positive adulteration result. You're looking for an actual oily residue or a distinctly different colored mark after the two minutes, not the fuel's natural tint.

If the outlet refuses to provide filter paper or claims not to have it, that itself is grounds for a complaint — it's mandatory equipment they're required to keep.

## Test 2: The density test (more reliable, slightly more involved)

This is the test I'd trust more if I genuinely suspected something was off, because it gives you an actual number instead of a visual judgment call.

Every fuel has a defined density range set by BIS standards. Petrol should sit between roughly 720-775 kg/m³ at 15°C, and diesel runs noticeably higher, around 820-845 kg/m³. Every petrol pump is required to keep a density chart, a calibrated hydrometer, a thermometer, and a small measuring jar (roughly 500ml) specifically for this test.

**Step 1** — Ask the attendant for a density check.

**Step 2** — A sample is drawn from the nozzle into the jar, filled about three-quarters full.

**Step 3** — The hydrometer is dipped in, giving a density reading, and the thermometer gives the temperature at that moment (density readings need temperature correction, since fuel expands slightly when warm).

**Step 4** — Compare that reading against the density chart displayed at the pump, and against what's logged in the pump's daily register for that fuel batch. The reading on the instrument and the register entry should match.

If the number falls outside the standard range, or doesn't match what's logged in the register, you've got a legitimate basis for a complaint.

One important thing I learned only after digging into this properly: since E20 (petrol blended with 20% ethanol) became the standard fuel in most of the country, some people assume any unusual density reading means ethanol adulteration. It doesn't — E20 is a government-mandated blend, formulated to still fall within that same 720-775 kg/m³ range. It's not the same thing as illegal adulteration, so don't panic over a normal E20 reading.

## Test 3: The 5-litre jar test (for short measurement, not adulteration)

This one's for the "am I actually getting the quantity I paid for" question, and it's separate from anything about fuel quality.

**Step 1** — Every pump is required to keep a government-certified 5-litre measuring jug, checked annually by the local Legal Metrology department for accuracy.

**Step 2** — Ask the attendant to dispense fuel into this jug instead of your tank.

**Step 3** — Check that the fuel fills exactly to the 5-litre mark, not visibly under or over.

**Step 4** — A deviation of up to about 25ml is generally considered within acceptable tolerance. Anything beyond that is a legitimate basis to complain.

## The simple stuff that catches most everyday short-changing

Honestly, most short measurement complaints I've read about don't even need the jar test — they come down to two basic checks that take five seconds:

- **Make sure the meter reads zero before fuel starts dispensing.** I've personally caught this once, where the attendant started filling before I'd actually confirmed the reset, and I only noticed because I'd made it a habit to glance at the display first.
- **Watch the meter while it's filling, not your phone.** This sounds almost too obvious to mention, but it's exactly when attendants at dishonest outlets count on you being distracted.

## What I actually do differently now

I switched back to my regular pump and kept a rough mileage log for about two months just to be sure it wasn't a coincidence. It wasn't — the numbers stayed consistent once I was back to my usual outlet. I also started sticking mostly to well-known company-owned or dealer outlets (Indian Oil, HP, BPCL, Shell) rather than switching pumps casually based on which one's two rupees cheaper that week, since the well-established brands tend to have more oversight and reputation risk if something's off.

I also now glance at the price display and meter zero every single time, which took maybe two weeks to become an actual habit instead of something I had to remind myself to do.

## Common mistakes people make with this

- **Mistaking petrol's natural pink tint for a stain in the filter paper test.** Know what a genuine oily residue looks like versus the fuel's own dye colour before assuming the worst.
- **Not asking for tests because they assume it's not allowed or will annoy the staff.** These are legal rights, not favours, and pump staff are required to accommodate the request.
- **Draining or using fuel you already suspect is adulterated, then complaining after the fact.** If you genuinely suspect a bad batch, preserve a sealed sample and the receipt before you do anything else — that's what any formal complaint process needs as evidence.
- **Skipping the receipt.** Always take the printed bill. Without it, you've got no paper trail if you need to escalate a complaint later.
- **Assuming one bad experience means every pump of that brand is suspect.** It's usually outlet-specific, tied to that particular dealer or a specific attendant, not the whole company's network.
- **Not checking a live price reference before you even get to the pump.** This isn't about adulteration, but it's a related habit — I keep a quick check on sites like [PetrolPriceIndia](https://petrolpriceindia.com/) before longer trips just so I know roughly what the day's rate should be and I'm not caught off guard by a pump quoting something unusual.

## If you actually find something wrong

Don't get into an argument at the pump. Note the pump's dealer/outlet code (displayed on the premises), the date and time, and ask for the complaint register — every pump is required to maintain one. Keep your receipt and, if it's an adulteration concern, a sealed sample of the fuel if the attendant allows it. Then escalate through the oil company's official customer care channel, or the district Legal Metrology department if it's a measurement issue. Consumer complaint portals and even the local petrol pump dealers' association (contactable through your city's association, which tends to take these complaints more seriously than you'd expect) are other routes people have used successfully.

## Final thoughts

I still don't know for certain whether that pump near my old office was actually adulterating fuel or if something else entirely explained the mileage drop. What changed is that I stopped shrugging off a weird pattern and actually learned what I'm entitled to ask for at any pump in the country. The tests themselves take two minutes and cost nothing. The habit of actually using them, that's the part that took a while to build.

If your mileage ever drops for no obvious mechanical reason, don't just assume it's your engine getting old. Sometimes it's worth asking a few questions at the pump first.
