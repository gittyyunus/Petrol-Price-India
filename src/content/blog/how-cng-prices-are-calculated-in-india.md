A friend who just switched his car to CNG called me last week, annoyed. "I thought CNG was supposed to be the cheap, tax-free option," he said. "So why did the price still jump ₹3 overnight, same as petrol does?"

That question stuck with me, because I'd assumed the same thing once — that CNG somehow floats outside the whole tax mess that makes petrol and diesel so expensive. It doesn't. It's just taxed differently, and the pricing has its own quirks that nobody really explains in one place.

So I went digging — through PPAC data, city gas distributor pricing pages, and a few tax notices — to actually understand what goes into a kilogram of CNG. Here's what I found, including the part where my first assumption turned out to be wrong.

## The assumption everyone gets wrong (including me, at first)

Here's the mix-up: a lot of people assume CNG runs on GST, the same "one nation, one tax" system that covers most goods in India. It doesn't. Natural gas — along with crude oil, petrol, diesel, and jet fuel — was deliberately left out of GST when it rolled out in 2017, and it's still outside it today.

So CNG isn't GST-free in some special environmentally-friendly way. It's taxed through the older system: central excise duty plus state VAT, layered on top of the actual cost of the gas. That's structurally closer to how petrol works than most people realize — it's just that the rates, and one extra ingredient (the raw gas cost itself), work differently.

## What actually makes up the price of CNG

Once you break it down, a kilogram of CNG is built from four pieces:

**1. Base gas cost.** This is the biggest chunk by far — usually somewhere around three-quarters of the price. City gas distributors (CGDs) like IGL, MGL, or Adani Gas buy natural gas either from domestic sources under the government's Administered Price Mechanism (APM), which is cheaper, or from imported LNG, which costs more and moves with global gas prices. How much of each a city's distributor uses directly affects what you pay at the pump.

**2. Compression and operating costs.** Turning natural gas into CNG means compressing it to over 200 bar of pressure, which takes real electricity and infrastructure. This usually adds another meaningful slice to the cost — running the compressors, maintaining the stations, that sort of thing.

**3. Central excise duty.** Unlike petrol, where excise is a flat per-litre amount, CNG carries a percentage-based excise duty from the central government — historically in the neighborhood of 14%, though it's worth checking the current rate since this has been a topic of ongoing policy debate (more on that below).

**4. State VAT.** This is where the big city-to-city swings come from. States set their own VAT on natural gas, and the range is wide — some states keep it in the single digits to encourage CNG adoption, while others charge upwards of 20%+. This is layered on top of everything above it, so a higher-VAT state doesn't just add a flat amount, it taxes an already-taxed subtotal.

## A rough example, so this isn't just abstract

Say the base gas cost plus compression and operating costs for a kilogram of CNG comes out to around ₹60. Add roughly 14% central excise, and you're at about ₹68.40. Now apply state VAT — if one state charges 5%, that's ₹71.80 final; if a neighboring state charges 15%, that same ₹68.40 subtotal becomes ₹78.70.

That's a real gap, purely from the VAT difference, even though the underlying gas cost and excise were identical. It's a big part of why you'll sometimes see CNG priced noticeably differently in cities that are barely an hour's drive apart — it's not really about "better" or "worse" gas, it's the state's VAT rate.

I'd treat these numbers as illustrative rather than exact — rates get revised, and the split between domestic APM gas and pricier imported LNG varies by city and by month, which is exactly why identical-looking numbers can shift without much warning. If you want to check where your city actually sits, sites that track this daily — [CNG price by city in India](/cng-price-in-india/) is one place I keep bookmarked — are more reliable than trying to back-calculate it yourself from a bill.

## How to actually check this for yourself

If you want to verify this instead of taking anyone's word for it (including mine):

**Step 1** — Check your city gas distributor's own pricing page. IGL, MGL, Gujarat Gas, and most others publish current CNG rates directly, and sometimes break out recent hikes with a reason attached.

**Step 2** — Cross-check against PPAC (ppac.gov.in), which tracks VAT and tax rates on petroleum and gas products state by state.

**Step 3** — Don't assume last month's number is this month's number. CNG prices move more often than people expect, especially when international LNG prices swing — which they've been doing a fair bit given global gas market volatility.

**Step 4** — If you're comparing two cities, check whether the difference is coming from VAT or from the domestic-vs-imported gas mix. They're not the same lever, and conflating them is an easy mistake to make (I made it, initially assuming a price gap was "just tax" when part of it was actually gas sourcing).

## Common mistakes people make with CNG pricing

**Assuming CNG is tax-free or GST-based.** It's neither — it's excise duty plus state VAT, same broad category as petrol and diesel, just with different numbers and one more variable (the gas sourcing mix).

**Comparing CNG price hikes directly to petrol/diesel hikes.** They don't move in lockstep. CNG pricing responds more to international LNG costs and the domestic-import gas ratio a distributor is working with, while petrol/diesel respond more to crude oil benchmarks.

**Assuming a state with low VAT always means the cheapest CNG.** VAT is only one of four layers. A state with low VAT but a distributor leaning heavily on imported LNG can still end up pricier than a state with slightly higher VAT but access to more domestic gas.

**Not accounting for city-level differences within the same state.** Different CGDs operating in the same state can still post different prices, since their individual gas-sourcing contracts and operating costs aren't identical.

**Treating one month's price as permanent.** With ongoing policy discussion around eventually bringing natural gas fully under GST — something that's been debated for years without full consensus — rates and structures here are more likely to shift than petrol/diesel's more settled framework.

## Final thoughts

The "CNG is the cheap, tax-light option" idea isn't entirely wrong — it's often still less expensive per kilometer than petrol — but it's not because CNG dodges taxation. It's taxed through basically the same central-excise-plus-state-VAT structure as petrol and diesel, it's just built on top of a genuinely different raw material cost that swings with global gas markets and each city's sourcing mix. For what that price gap is actually worth in rupees per kilometre — and when it stops being worth it — see [why CNG is better than petrol](/blog/why-cng-is-better-than-petrol/).

Next time someone tells you CNG jumped in price "for no reason," you'll actually be able to explain which of the four pieces moved — and that's a lot more useful than just shrugging and blaming the pump.
