**The short version:** a petrol pump in India costs roughly **₹1 crore to ₹3 crore all-in** for most applicants, and the single biggest swing factor is whether you already own suitable land. The physical build — canopy, underground storage tanks, dispensers, civil work, electrical and fire safety — typically runs **₹60 lakh to ₹1.2 crore**. On top of that sit the security deposit to the oil company, licences (PESO, Legal Metrology, Fire, pollution consent, municipal), the first fill of stock and working capital: another **₹20-50 lakh**.

Then comes the part most "how to open a petrol pump" articles leave out. You do not earn a margin on the price of fuel. You earn a **fixed commission of about ₹3.6-3.8 per litre of petrol and ₹2.5 per litre of diesel** — a number set by the oil marketing companies (OMCs) and published by the government, not by you. Whether that commission ever repays ₹2 crore depends almost entirely on daily sales volume, and daily sales volume depends almost entirely on the site. That is the whole business in one sentence.

This article breaks down every cost head with realistic 2026 ranges, the eligibility and licensing gates you must clear before spending anything, and the arithmetic of what a pump actually returns — using the official commission data rather than optimism.

## 1. Why almost every number you find online is wrong

Search for "petrol pump investment" and you will find figures ranging from ₹15 lakh to ₹5 crore, often on the same page. The reason is that the question is being answered at three different levels, and nobody says which:

| What the article usually means | Typical number | What's actually included |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum "investment capacity" to be *eligible* to apply | ₹25 lakh (regular), ₹12 lakh (rural) | Not a cost at all — it's a bank-balance test in the OMC advertisement |
| Cost of the built outlet (construction + equipment) | ₹60 lakh – ₹1.2 crore | Land excluded, working capital excluded |
| Total project cost including land, deposits, licences, first stock | ₹1.2 crore – ₹3 crore | What you actually need to arrange |

The eligibility figure of ₹25 lakh is the most commonly quoted and the most misunderstood. It is not what a pump costs. It is the minimum financial capacity an applicant must *demonstrate* — in savings, fixed deposits, NSC, bonds, demat shares or mutual funds — to be allowed to apply for a regular (non-rural) outlet. Cash and jewellery don't count, current-account balances don't count, and for shares, bonds and mutual funds only 60% of the value is typically reckoned. If you cannot show that money, the application is rejected before anyone looks at your land.

So: budget on the third line, and treat the first line as an entry ticket.

## 2. The three cost buckets, head by head

Every rupee you spend falls into one of three buckets: land, physical build, and everything else. Here is a realistic breakdown for a standard 4-dispenser, 2-product (petrol + diesel) retail outlet on a reasonable site in 2026. Ranges reflect the difference between a rural plot in a low-cost district and a highway site near a city.

### Bucket 1: Land

| Item | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Plot size norms | 800-1,200 sq m urban, 1,500-2,500 sq m highway | Varies by OMC and by advertisement; frontage and road access matter as much as area |
| Buying the land | ₹20 lakh – ₹1.5 crore+ | Entirely location-dependent; this is the line that makes "the cost of a petrol pump" a meaningless single number |
| Land lease route | ₹25,000 – ₹1.5 lakh/month | Lease of 15-30 years is accepted by OMCs; you avoid the capital outlay but carry rent for the life of the dealership |
| Change of land use (agricultural → commercial) | ₹1 lakh – ₹5 lakh, plus 6-18 months of time | Must be done *before* applying, not after |
| Site development: cutting/filling, compaction, retaining wall, 1.5 m compound wall, approach road | ₹5 lakh – ₹20 lakh | OMCs require the plot handed over developed to road level, layer-wise compacted |

The honest advice here is that if you don't already own suitable land on a decent road, land is the cost that decides whether this project is viable at all. Buying a plot purely to open a pump usually makes the payback arithmetic collapse. If you own it, your opportunity cost is the rent you could have earned instead — which is why many second-generation dealers quietly treat the land as part of the family's existing balance sheet rather than a new expense.

### Bucket 2: The physical build

This is the "cost of construction", and it is tightly specified. You cannot build a pump however you like — the OMC issues approved drawings and an approved equipment list, and the outlet is commissioned against them. Deviating to save money is the fastest way to have the outlet rejected at inspection.

| Item | Typical range | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Underground storage tanks (UST) | ₹12 lakh – ₹25 lakh | Double-walled, 10,000-20,000 litre compartments per product, leak monitoring in the interstitial space |
| Submersible turbine pump + Automatic Tank Gauge probe | ₹2 lakh – ₹5 lakh | The pump is inside the tank, not in the dispenser — see [how a petrol pump works](/blog/how-petrol-pump-works/) |
| Dispensing units | ₹6 lakh – ₹12 lakh each | 4 to 8 dispensers is normal; OMC-approved models only, E20-compatible specification |
| Steel canopy with signage and lighting | ₹8 lakh – ₹20 lakh | Foundation, columns, cladding, brand signage — structural design must handle wind uplift |
| Civil work: RCC forecourt, dispenser islands, forecourt drainage, sumps | ₹15 lakh – ₹35 lakh | The forecourt is a purpose-built reinforced surface, not ordinary paving |
| Office cabin, cashier counter, washroom, staff room | ₹5 lakh – ₹12 lakh | |
| Electrical: transformer/connection, earthing, flameproof fittings, lighting | ₹5 lakh – ₹12 lakh | Hazard-zone equipment is explosion-proof by requirement |
| Fire safety: foam system, extinguishers, sand buckets, fire water tank | ₹3 lakh – ₹8 lakh | Specified by PESO and the Fire Services NOC |
| DG set (backup generator) | ₹1.5 lakh – ₹4 lakh | An outlet that can't pump during a power cut stops earning |
| Total construction and equipment | **₹60 lakh – ₹1.2 crore** | Land and working capital excluded |

Two technical notes that cost people money when they skip them:

- **Everything must be E20-rated.** India's petrol is now a 20% ethanol blend, and ethanol attacks ordinary rubber, seals and some metals. Tanks, hoses, nozzles, seals and gaskets specified for the outlet must be ethanol-compatible. Installing cheaper, non-rated equipment and then being told to replace it at commissioning is a real and expensive mistake — the chemistry is covered in [what E20 petrol actually does to your car](/blog/what-is-e20-petrol/).
- **Accuracy equipment is not optional.** The dispenser's meter, pulser and electronic head sit behind Legal Metrology seals, and new equipment is verified and sealed before commissioning. Understanding why helps: see [how a petrol pump works](/blog/how-petrol-pump-works/) and [the tests you can legally ask for at any pump](/blog/why-my-motorcycle-mileage-dropped-fuel-pump-tests/).

### Bucket 3: Deposits, licences, stock and working capital

These are the costs that turn "₹60 lakh for construction" into a ₹1.5-3 crore project, and they are the ones most first-time applicants under-budget.

| Item | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Application fee | ₹2,000 – ₹6,000 | Non-refundable, paid online to the OMC |
| Security deposit / bank guarantee to OMC | ₹2 lakh – ₹6 lakh | Paid on acceptance of the Letter of Intent; refunded at the end of the agreement |
| PESO licence (petroleum storage) | ₹50,000 – ₹2 lakh | Includes application, inspection and consultant fees; must precede any storage |
| Legal Metrology verification and sealing | ₹5,000 per nozzle, plus testing costs | Government Approved Test Centres were authorised to verify dispensers in 2026 at ₹5,000 per nozzle for petrol and diesel |
| Fire Services NOC | ₹25,000 – ₹1 lakh | State-dependent |
| Pollution consent — Consent to Establish and Consent to Operate | ₹1 lakh – ₹3 lakh | From the State Pollution Control Board; both stages needed |
| Municipal trade licence, building plan approval, signboard permissions | ₹50,000 – ₹2 lakh | Local body dependent |
| GST registration, professional and documentation charges | ₹50,000 – ₹1.5 lakh | |
| First stock of fuel and initial tank filling | ₹15 lakh – ₹40 lakh | Roughly a tank-full per product at dealer price |
| Working capital margin (staff, electricity, consumables for the first months) | ₹10 lakh – ₹25 lakh | OMC supplies fuel on a credit cycle, so you don't need to prepay for all sales — but you need a buffer |
| Contingency | ₹5 lakh – ₹15 lakh | Costs overrun; the OMC inspection cycle always finds something |
| **Total** | **₹50 lakh – ₹1.2 crore** | Excluding land |

Adding the three buckets gives the range quoted at the top: **₹1.2 crore to ₹3 crore**, with a rural outlet you already own land for sitting at the bottom and a highway site you had to buy near a city sitting at the top. A Shell-run outlet in India, for comparison, has been reported at roughly ₹75 lakh to ₹3 crore of dealer investment excluding land — the same shape of number.

One thing that confuses people: in some OMC schemes and for some reserved or difficult locations, the oil company offers **cost-of-construction (COC) assistance** — effectively funding part of the civil work against agreed terms. This is scheme-specific, changes with every advertisement, and never appears in generic articles. If you are applying for a specific site, read that site's notification line by line rather than assuming.

## 3. The eligibility gate — clear it before you spend anything

Money spent before eligibility is confirmed is money wasted, because the OMC screens applicants *before* the site visit, the inspection or any construction.

| Criterion | Typical requirement |
|---|---|
| Citizenship and residency | Indian citizen, resident under Income Tax rules |
| Age | 21-55 years at application (some OMCs go to 60) |
| Education | Rural SC/ST/OBC: 10th pass; other rural and open-category: 10+2; open category outside rural areas: graduate, or CA/CS/CMA, or a diploma in engineering |
| Financial capacity | ₹25 lakh for a regular outlet, ₹12 lakh for a rural one — in documented, liquid-ish assets |
| Land | Owned or long-term leased, clear title, correct frontage and road access, correct land-use classification |
| Disqualifications | Government employees and close relatives, criminal convictions, tax defaults, undisclosed existing dealerships |

Land is where most applications die. Title defects — un-done mutations, khatauni in someone else's name, agricultural classification, litigation, an old unregistered sale deed — are the common killers. Get a proper title search and the change of land use done *before* you apply; conversion alone can take 6-18 months.

And the uncomfortable structural fact: **you do not choose your pump, the OMC does.** Locations are advertised, applications are screened, and the final selection is by draw of lots (with bidding used for some premium sites). Nobody — no agent, no consultant, no politician — can guarantee you an allotment. Which brings us to the biggest hidden cost of all.

## 4. The scam tax: the most expensive line item in this business

There is an entire industry selling "petrol pump dealership allotments" for ₹5-20 lakh in "facilitation" fees. It is the most reliable way to lose money in this sector, and it works because the real process is genuinely opaque and slow.

The rules that keep you safe:

- **Real advertisements appear only on the OMC's own website and in leading newspapers.** IndianOil, Bharat Petroleum and Hindustan Petroleum each publish location-specific notifications; apply only through the official portal named in that advertisement.
- **No one sells allotments.** Selection is by lot or by documented bidding against advertised terms. An agent who "knows someone in the selection committee" is describing a crime and taking your money.
- **Application fees are small and paid online to the OMC.** Any demand for lakhs in cash, before or after the draw, is fraud.
- **A Letter of Intent is not an allotment.** It is conditional on security deposit, land verification, licences and construction within the stipulated period. People who buy "LOIs" from intermediaries discover this after paying.

A legitimate consultant charging a documented fee for paperwork, title search and project-report preparation is a different thing from a broker selling influence. Budget ₹50,000-₹2 lakh for honest professional help; never budget a rupee for the other kind.

## 5. The timeline: 12 to 24 months of carrying costs

Every stage below has a cost attached, even when no invoice arrives.

| Stage | Typical duration | Cost impact |
|---|---|---|
| Watching for the advertisement and applying | 30-45 day window | Application fee |
| OMC scrutiny and site inspection | 1-3 months | Survey, travel, document rectification |
| Selection, Letter of Intent, security deposit | 2-6 months | Deposit payable, land title work |
| PESO licence, fire NOC, pollution consent, municipal approvals | 2-6 months (overlapping) | Fees, consultants, plan approvals |
| Construction and installation | 4-8 months | The bulk of the money |
| Commissioning and first sale | 1-2 months | Staff hiring, training, first stock |

Two carrying costs deserve their own line: **interest on borrowed capital** and **land rent** if you're leasing. If you take a ₹1.5 crore term loan at around 10%, that is roughly ₹12.5 lakh a year of interest before you have sold a single litre. Many applicants plan construction and financing at the same time; almost all of them underestimate the interest accrued during the 12-24 months before commissioning.

## 6. The earning side: what a pump actually pays you

This is the part that determines whether the cost was worth it, so it's worth being precise. Dealers do not set prices and do not keep a margin. They receive a **commission per litre** fixed by the OMCs and published by the Ministry of Petroleum through PPAC. The latest revision on the official record (1 December 2024) reads:

| Product | Commission formula | Roughly per litre |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol (MS) | ₹3,144.03 per kilolitre + 0.870% of product billable price | ≈ ₹3.60 - ₹3.80 |
| Diesel (HSD) | ₹2,332.51 per kilolitre + 0.266% of product billable price | ≈ ₹2.45 - ₹2.60 |

Two deductions and one addition change the real number:

- **Licence Fee Recovery (LFR)** is deducted by the OMC — about ₹443.50 per kilolitre of petrol and ₹199.97 per kilolitre of diesel at "a" category sites (roughly half that at "b" sites), with 18% GST on top. Call it ₹0.50-0.52 per litre of petrol on a prime site.
- **Slab-based incentives** are paid back as credit notes to outlets that cross volume thresholds — this is genuinely worth money, and it's why high-volume sites earn disproportionately.
- Non-fuel business — CNG sales, lubricants, the small shop, ATMs, EV charging — sits on top and is increasingly where a decent part of the profit comes from.

Note what is *not* in that table: a percentage of the pump price. When petrol crosses ₹110 a litre, the dealer still earns the same ₹3.7 a litre. And when the OMC revises prices daily — the mechanism is covered in [why petrol prices change at 6 AM](/blog/why-petrol-prices-change-at-6am-and-how-omcs-calculate-them/) — the dealer's own income does not move. The commission is fixed while costs are not, which is exactly why dealers protest when wages, power and rent rise.

## 7. The break-even arithmetic, done honestly

Let's assume a well-run outlet with an investment of about ₹2 crore, and see what it takes.

**Assumptions:** 60% petrol, 40% diesel by volume; commission at ₹3.70 and ₹2.50 per litre respectively; LFR and GST deducted; staff of 8-10 (manager, cashier, attendants, night guard) at prevailing minimum wages; electricity for dispensers, lighting, air compressor and a DG set; monthly maintenance and consumables; no rent (owned land); construction funded partly by a term loan.

| Daily sales | Monthly litres | Gross commission | After LFR | Fixed operating costs | Surplus before interest & tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,500 L (weak rural) | 45,000 | ~₹1.38 lakh | ~₹1.14 lakh | ₹2.5 - ₹3.5 lakh | **Negative** |
| 3,500 L (average) | 1,05,000 | ~₹3.2 lakh | ~₹2.7 lakh | ₹2.8 - ₹3.5 lakh | **₹0 - ₹0.5 lakh** |
| 8,000 L (good urban) | 2.4 lakh | ~₹7.3 lakh | ~₹6.3 lakh | ₹3.2 - ₹4 lakh | **₹2.3 - ₹3 lakh** |
| 12,000 L (highway) | 3.6 lakh | ~₹11 lakh | ~₹9.6 lakh | ₹3.5 - ₹4.5 lakh | **₹5 - ₹6 lakh** |

Three conclusions fall straight out of that table:

1. **Break-even is around 3,000-4,000 litres a day.** Below that, staff and electricity alone can eat the entire commission — which is the situation a large number of rural outlets are quietly in.
2. **The business is volume, not margin.** A 30-paise commission improvement is worth less than a 10% lift in daily sales. Site selection is the profit decision; everything else is hygiene.
3. **Debt changes the answer.** Fund ₹1.5 crore of that ₹2 crore at 10% and roughly ₹12.5 lakh a year goes in interest — turning a ₹35-45 lakh annual surplus into ₹20-30 lakh, and pushing payback from about 5 years to 7-8.

A realistic payback for a genuinely good site is therefore **4-8 years**, on a dealership agreement that typically runs for a defined term and is renewable subject to performance. Anything promising recovery in 2-3 years is assuming a volume most outlets never see.

One more number worth carrying: **wetstock loss.** Fuel that quietly disappears between delivery and sale — evaporation, leak, metering drift, water in the tank — is a real cost at every outlet, and at 0.1-0.3% of throughput it can be worth several lakh rupees a year on a high-volume site. It's a management problem, not a mystery; the storage and measurement side is explained in [how a petrol pump works](/blog/how-petrol-pump-works/).

## 8. Running costs that catch new dealers out

- **Staff.** A pump runs long hours, every day of the year. Attendants, a cashier, a manager and a night guard are not optional, and wage boards vary significantly by state — budget ₹1.5-2.5 lakh a month in most states, more in the metros.
- **Electricity.** Dispensers, canopy lighting, air compressors, the office and the DG set's fuel: ₹30,000-₹80,000 a month depending on scale and tariff.
- **Digital payment charges.** A large share of fuel sales is now UPI or card. Where merchant discount rates are not reimbursed, even 1% of a ₹10 lakh monthly digital turnover is ₹10,000 straight off the commission — and disputes over who bears MDR have been a standing feature of dealer-OMC relations.
- **Metering and metrology compliance.** Periodic re-verification, sealing, filter and nozzle servicing, hose replacement. Skimping here shows up as complaints, penalties and lost fuel — and the legal position is covered in [the fuel pump tests guide](/blog/why-my-motorcycle-mileage-dropped-fuel-pump-tests/).
- **Inventory discipline.** Tanks are refilled by tanker deliveries scheduled against your ordering; ordering badly either strands capital or risks a stock-out on a festival weekend.

A pump built in 2026 is not the same asset as one built in 2016, and the extras are increasingly mandatory in practice:

| Addition | Typical cost | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| CNG dispensing (cascades, dispenser, PESO approval) | ₹30 lakh – ₹80 lakh | CNG's margin structure differs from liquid fuel — see [how CNG prices are calculated](/blog/how-cng-prices-are-calculated-in-india/) |

| Air, water, wash bay, convenience store | ₹3 lakh – ₹15 lakh | Non-fuel revenue with higher margin per rupee of turnover |
| Tyre/inflation, ATMs | ₹1-3 lakh | |

The strategic pressure is real. As more of the fleet shifts to CNG and electric — the five-year economics are covered in [EV vs petrol car ownership cost](/blog/ev-vs-petrol-car-5-year-ownership-cost/) — the litres-per-day assumption behind your payback plan starts drifting. An outlet that can't serve CNG or charging is an outlet that loses part of its catchment every year.

## 10. Should you do it? An honest filter

Open a petrol pump if:

- You already own suitable land on a road with genuine traffic — this is worth more than any other single factor.
- You can fund ₹60 lakh-₹1 crore of equity without borrowing all of it, and carry interest during a 12-24 month gestation.
- Someone in the family can run the outlet personally, day to day. OMCs explicitly prefer hands-on operators, and absentee ownership is where compliance failures and pilferage start.
- You are treating it as a long-term, low-single-digit-return asset rather than a quick win.

Think hard if:

- You'd be buying land specifically for the project.
- The entire ₹2 crore would be borrowed.
- Your only evidence of viability is what an agent promised.
- The site's traffic is a projection with no history behind it.

## 11. Frequently asked questions

### How much does a petrol pump cost in India in 2026?

Expect ₹1.2 crore to ₹3 crore all-in for a standard outlet: ₹60 lakh-₹1.2 crore for construction and equipment, ₹20-50 lakh for deposit, licences, first stock and working capital, and land on top of that — anywhere from nothing (family land you already own) to over ₹1.5 crore on a prime highway site. Rural outlets you already own land for can be brought in near the bottom of that range.

### How much do petrol pump owners actually earn per litre?

About ₹3.60-3.80 per litre of petrol and ₹2.45-2.60 per litre of diesel at current rates, before deducting licence fee recovery, and with slab incentives added back at higher volumes. That is the whole margin — the owner does not keep anything from the tax or base price components of the pump rate.

### Is a petrol pump profitable?

At the right site, yes — a highway or busy urban outlet doing 8,000-12,000 litres a day can throw off ₹2.5-6 lakh a month before interest and tax, with payback in 4-8 years. At an average site doing under 4,000 litres a day, the outlet often barely covers staff and electricity. Volume is the whole game.

### Can I open a petrol pump on my own land?

Yes — and it's the single biggest advantage you can have. Ownership or a long-term lease (typically 15-30 years) of a plot meeting the advertised size, frontage, access and land-use norms is one of the two eligibility gates. The other is being selected in the OMC's advertisement-driven process, which is by draw of lots or documented bidding.

### How long does it take to get a petrol pump dealership?

Typically 12-24 months from application to first sale — including advertisement, scrutiny, site inspection, selection, Letter of Intent, PESO and other licences, construction and commissioning. Anyone promising you a pump in three months is not describing the real process.

### Do I need any licence besides the OMC's approval?

Yes, several, and all of them are mandatory: a PESO licence for storing petroleum, Legal Metrology verification and sealing of the dispensers, a Fire Services NOC, Consent to Establish and Consent to Operate from the State Pollution Control Board, municipal trade licence and building plan approval, and GST registration. Budget ₹4-10 lakh and several months for the lot.

### Can I sell or transfer my petrol pump dealership later?

You can exit, but the dealership is contractual — a transfer requires OMC approval and typically attracts a transfer fee, and the outgoing dealer's compliance record follows the outlet. Dealerships are also valued on their volume, site quality and agreement standing, which is why good outlets change hands at a premium well above construction cost.

### Is this business at risk from EVs?

Slowly, yes — which is why the smartest new outlets are being built with CNG and EV charging as core infrastructure rather than afterthoughts. Petrol demand in India is broadly plateauing while vehicle kilometres keep growing; the outlets that adapt capture the growing segments, and those that don't compete purely on litres. The long-run consumer economics are in [EV vs petrol car ownership cost](/blog/ev-vs-petrol-car-5-year-ownership-cost/).

## Final thoughts

The question "how much does it cost to open a petrol pump" doesn't have one answer, because a pump isn't a product you buy — it's a site-specific, OMC-allotted, licence-heavy, volume-dependent business. What can be said precisely is the shape of it: **₹1.2-3 crore to build and open, ₹3.6 a litre to earn, and roughly 3,500 litres a day before you stop losing money.**

Everything that separates a good dealership from a bad one happens before construction: the land, the traffic on the road in front of it, the honesty of whoever is helping you apply, and the financing you arranged for the 12-24 months when you earn nothing. Get those four right and the rest of the business — commission per litre, wetstock discipline, compliance, staff — is manageable arithmetic. Get them wrong and no amount of care at the counter repairs it.

If the money side is what you're really trying to understand, start with [how fuel excise duty and state VAT are calculated](/blog/how-fuel-excise-duty-and-state-vat-are-calculated/) — that's where roughly half of every rupee a customer hands over goes — and check today's rates on our [petrol price tracker](/petrol-price-in-india/). And if you end up behind the counter, [how a petrol pump works](/blog/how-petrol-pump-works/) explains the machine your livelihood will depend on, tank to nozzle.
