# State-Wise Petrol Price Ranking 2026: Which Indian States Pay the Most (and Least) Tax on Fuel

Last year I drove from Indore to a friend's place just across the Madhya Pradesh–Rajasthan border, and when I stopped to fill up on the Rajasthan side out of pure habit, the guy at the pump told me the rate. I actually made him repeat it because it didn't match what I'd paid the same morning back home. Same petrol, same day, roughly 40 km apart — and a real difference on the meter.

I remember thinking, okay, this can't just be "transport cost," because 40 km of extra trucking doesn't explain that kind of gap. That one fill-up sent me down a rabbit hole of understanding exactly how petrol pricing works in India, and honestly, once you see the breakup, you can't unsee it every time you're at a pump.

This piece is everything I've figured out since — the real reason your neighbour in another state pays a different price than you, which states are brutal on the wallet, which ones are surprisingly kind, and a couple of things I wish I'd known before that road trip.

## It's not really "petrol price" — it's a tax structure wearing a petrol price costume

Here's the first thing that surprised me: petrol and diesel are still outside GST. Everything else in India — your phone, your groceries, your Swiggy order — got folded into one tax system back in 2017. Fuel didn't, because state governments get to set their own VAT on it, and that VAT is a massive chunk of state revenue. Nobody's in a hurry to give that up voluntarily.

So what you pay at the pump is basically four things stacked on top of each other:

- **Base price** — what it actually costs to refine and deliver the fuel to the dealer, tied to international crude rates and the rupee-dollar exchange rate
- **Central excise duty** — a fixed amount the central government charges, same across the whole country
- **Dealer commission** — a cut for the petrol pump owner, roughly 3–4% of the price
- **State VAT / sales tax** — this is the one that changes everything, because every state sets its own rate

The base price and central excise are identical whether you're in Kerala or Kashmir. The VAT is where states go their own way, and that's the entire reason your fuel bill changes the second you cross a state line.

Quick real-world context: after the crude price spike triggered by the West Asia tensions earlier in 2026, the central government actually cut excise duty by ₹10 a litre in March to soften the blow — bringing petrol's special duty down to ₹3 and diesel's down to zero. That helped keep retail prices from jumping further, but it didn't touch the state VAT layer at all, which is still the biggest swing factor between states.

## Why some states charge so much more

I used to assume higher fuel price = richer state charging more because they can. Turns out it's more about how dependent that state's budget is on fuel revenue versus other income sources.

States that lean heavily on petrol and diesel VAT for their own funds tend to keep rates high, and a few even add extra layers on top of the standard VAT — things like a road development cess, or an additional non-recoverable surcharge stacked on the VAT itself. Bihar and West Bengal are known for this; they charge an extra surcharge on top of VAT that isn't refundable anywhere in the tax chain.

There's also a quirk that genuinely annoyed me once I understood it: in states like Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana, and a few Union Territories, VAT is applied not just on the base fuel price but on the dealer's commission too. So you're paying tax on the tax base plus tax on someone else's margin. It's a small amount per litre, but across a full tank, and across a year of driving, it adds up.

## The states that hit hardest — and the ones that go easy on you

Based on how the tax structure has played out over the last couple of years, a rough pattern holds pretty consistently:

**Higher-tax end:** Telangana has repeatedly topped the charts with among the highest VAT rates on petrol in the country, sitting well above 30%. Andhra Pradesh isn't far behind, and Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh both tend to sit in that upper bracket too, partly because of additional cess layers on top of the base VAT.

**Lower-tax end:** Andaman & Nicobar Islands is consistently the cheapest place to fill up in India, with a sales tax rate in the single digits compared to the 25–35% range in the high-tax states. Puducherry and a few smaller Union Territories also tend to sit noticeably lower, since UTs generally have more flexibility to keep rates competitive to attract footfall and business.

The gap between the two ends isn't small. On the same day, petrol has been known to cost around ₹87 a litre in the Andamans while touching well over ₹110 in some western and southern metros. That's not a rounding difference — that's a genuinely different cost of driving depending on your pin code.

## A rough calculation, so this isn't just abstract numbers

Say the base price for a litre of petrol (before any tax) is around ₹58, based on current crude and refining costs. Add central excise duty of a few rupees, add dealer commission of roughly ₹4, and you're at maybe ₹65–66 before state tax touches it.

Now apply a 20% VAT state versus a 33% VAT state:

- At 20% VAT, that ₹65 becomes roughly ₹78 a litre
- At 33% VAT, the same ₹65 becomes roughly ₹86–87 a litre

That's close to a ₹9 difference per litre from VAT rate alone, before you even factor in extra cesses some states pile on. Fill a 40-litre tank and you're looking at a ₹350+ difference purely because of which side of a state line you're standing on.

## How I actually use this now (step by step)

1. **Before any long road trip, I check prices in the state I'm leaving and the state I'm entering.** A quick search or the fuel price section on apps like the IndianOil app, or sending "RSP <city code>" to 92249 92249, gives you the day's rate for any city.
2. **If I know I'm crossing into a cheaper-tax state within the next day or two, I don't top up fully before leaving.** I fill just enough to get me across comfortably and top up properly once I'm there.
3. **If it's the reverse — heading into a higher-tax state — I fill up fully before I leave**, especially for petrol, since the per-litre gap tends to be bigger for petrol than diesel.
4. **For regular city driving, I stopped worrying about daily fluctuations entirely.** Prices move a few paise most days based on crude and currency movement — chasing that is a waste of time and fuel. The state-level gap is the one that actually matters for planning.
5. **I also stopped assuming rural or smaller towns are automatically cheaper.** Transport cost to remote areas can sometimes offset any tax advantage, so it's genuinely worth checking rather than assuming.

## Mistakes I made before I understood any of this

I used to think fuel price differences were about how "developed" a state was, which is just wrong — it's a tax policy choice, not a wealth indicator. I also assumed dealer commission was tiny and irrelevant, until I realised some states literally tax that commission too. And for the longest time I thought GST applied to fuel like everything else, which led to a genuinely confusing argument with a friend who insisted diesel should be way cheaper post-GST — it never went into GST at all, and probably won't for a while, since states would need to agree to give up a revenue stream that funds a meaningful chunk of their budgets.

One more thing worth knowing: don't expect central excise duty cuts to always show up at the pump. When the government trimmed excise duty this year to ease pressure on oil companies, it mostly helped state-run retailers absorb losses rather than translating into an immediate, visible price drop for consumers. Retail prices are sticky — they don't always move down as fast as they move up.

## Final thoughts

None of this means you need to become a tax expert before every fill-up. But if you drive across state borders even occasionally — for work, for family visits, for road trips — knowing that the VAT layer is where the real money difference sits changes how you plan. It's the difference between filling up out of habit and filling up with a bit of intention.

That one accidental fill-up in Rajasthan taught me more about how Indian fuel pricing actually works than any explainer article did at the time. Hopefully this saves you the confused conversation with the pump attendant and gets you straight to the useful part.
