A colleague bought a CNG Swift last month and sent me a photo of the instrument cluster before he had even crossed 500 km. "Thirty-two point eight," he wrote. "My petrol Swift never went above eighteen. CNG is nearly double, yaar."

I did not have the heart to tell him immediately that he was comparing kilometres per kilogram with kilometres per litre — two different denominators — and that the "nearly double" was mostly a unit trick. But his message stayed with me, because it is the single most common mistake I see in CNG conversations. Owners, salesmen, even some reviews compare 32 km/kg with 18 km/l as if they were the same scale, conclude that CNG is 70–80% more efficient, and then feel cheated when the actual fuel bill does not fall by that much.

The truth is more interesting than the myth. CNG does give you more kilometres per unit — but not because its engine is magically twice as efficient. It gives you more kilometres per kilogram because a kilogram of CNG carries roughly 40% more energy than a litre of petrol. Once you put both fuels on the same energy scale, the efficiency gap mostly disappears. What remains is a real but smaller set of engine advantages, and a much larger price advantage that has nothing to do with mileage at all.

This piece is about that distinction: what CNG's mileage number actually means, why it is higher, when the efficiency gain is real, when it is not, and how to measure your own car properly instead of trusting the brochure or the fuel gauge.

If you want the full ownership case — kit prices, payback, resale, safety and the factory-versus-retrofit decision — I have already worked that through in [why CNG is better than petrol in India](/blog/why-cng-is-better-than-petrol/). This article stays on the mileage question.

## The short answer

CNG shows a higher mileage number than petrol because of four things, in descending order of importance:

1. **The unit is different.** CNG is sold by the kilogram, petrol by the litre. One kilogram of CNG contains roughly the same energy as 1.35–1.45 litres of petrol, so a fair comparison is not 32 km/kg versus 18 km/l — it is about 23 km/l petrol-equivalent versus 18 km/l.
2. **CNG has a higher octane rating**, which lets a purpose-built CNG engine run a higher compression ratio and convert slightly more of that energy into motion. Most Indian CNG cars are bi-fuel — they run on both petrol and gas — and keep the petrol engine's compression ratio, so they leave most of this advantage unused.
3. **A gaseous fuel mixes more completely with air** and does not condense on cold intake walls the way liquid petrol does. That can make combustion cleaner and more consistent, especially in stop-start driving and during warm-up. The gain is real but usually small — a few percent, not tens of percent.
4. **CNG avoids some of petrol's small losses** — evaporative vapour losses, oil dilution, and the richer fuelling a cold petrol engine needs. Again, these are second-order effects, not the main event.

Then there is the part that actually changes your bank balance: **CNG is cheaper per unit of energy than petrol**, by a wide margin in most Indian cities. At the Delhi prices used in this article, a megajoule of CNG costs about ₹1.81 against ₹3.05 for petrol — roughly 40% less. That price gap, not a 40% efficiency gap, is why the CNG car is cheaper to run.

**The one-line version:** CNG's mileage number is higher mostly because a kilogram is a bigger unit than a litre. Its engine is roughly as efficient as a petrol engine — sometimes a little better, sometimes a little worse — but its fuel is significantly cheaper per unit of energy. The savings are real; the "double mileage" story is not.

## The unit trap: why 34 km/kg is not 34 km/l

Start with the numbers on the fuel itself. These are the properties that matter, using Indian fuel specifications and standard reference values:

| Property | Petrol | CNG |
|---|---|---|
| Physical state at normal temperature | Liquid | Gas, stored at 200–250 bar |
| Density | 720–775 kg/m³ (about 0.72–0.775 kg per litre) | About 0.72 kg/m³ at standard conditions; about 180 kg/m³ at 200 bar |
| Energy content, lower heating value | About 43.5–44.5 MJ/kg | About 46–50 MJ/kg |
| Energy per unit sold | **About 32–34.5 MJ per litre** | **About 46–50 MJ per kilogram** |
| Knock resistance (RON equivalent) | 91–95 | About 120–130 |
| Stoichiometric air-fuel ratio | About 14.7:1 by mass | About 17.2:1 by mass |
| Energy per litre of storage volume | About 32–34.5 MJ/L | About 9–10 MJ/L at 200 bar |
| How it is sold in India | Per litre | Per kilogram |

Two rows in that table do most of the work.

The first is **energy per unit sold**: a litre of petrol carries about 32–34.5 MJ, while a kilogram of CNG carries about 46–50 MJ. Divide one by the other and you get roughly **1.35–1.45** for typical Indian petrol and gas, with about **1.4** as a practical working average. One kilogram of CNG is therefore energy-equivalent to roughly 1.4 litres of petrol.

The second is **energy per litre of storage volume**: at 200 bar, CNG carries only about 9–10 MJ per litre of tank volume, against petrol's 32–34.5 MJ per litre. This is why CNG tanks are large, heavy and cylindrical, and why a CNG car's range is shorter than a petrol car's even though the fuel itself has more energy per kilogram. It also explains why "CNG has more energy" is only half true: it has more energy per kilogram, but far less per litre of space.

So when your CNG car says 32 km/kg, it is not saying "32 kilometres on the same amount of fuel that would give a petrol car 18 kilometres." It is saying "32 kilometres on 1 kilogram of gas, which contains the energy of about 1.4 litres of petrol." Convert it, and the picture changes completely:

**Petrol-equivalent km/l = CNG km/kg ÷ 1.4**

At 32 km/kg, that is about **22.9 km/l petrol-equivalent**. At 34 km/kg, it is about **24.3 km/l**. At 24 km/kg — a realistic city figure for many CNG hatchbacks — it is about **17.1 km/l**. Suddenly the CNG car is not doubling the petrol car. It is roughly matching it, sometimes beating it by a few percent, sometimes trailing it by a few percent.

### The reverse calculation that makes it obvious

Here is the same trick from the petrol side. A petrol hatchback that returns 24.35 km/l — the ARAI figure for the Maruti WagonR 1.0 — burns about 0.745 kg of petrol per litre. So its mileage per kilogram of petrol is:

**24.35 km/l ÷ 0.745 kg/l = 32.7 km/kg**

Read that again. The petrol WagonR, expressed in kilometres per kilogram, is a **32.7 km/kg car**. The CNG WagonR is rated at **34.05 km/kg**. The difference is about **4%**, not 40%.

That single calculation is the whole answer to "why does CNG give more mileage?" It gives more *per kilogram* because petrol's own per-kilogram number is also high — you just never see it, because petrol is sold by the litre.

### The same comparison across six real Indian cars

Here is the ARAI-certified comparison for six popular models, using the 1.4 conversion factor. These are laboratory figures, not real-world numbers, but the *ratio* between the petrol and CNG variants of the same model is what matters:

| Model (petrol and CNG variants) | Petrol ARAI (km/l) | CNG ARAI (km/kg) | CNG ÷ petrol | Petrol-equivalent (km/l) | Energy-efficiency difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maruti WagonR 1.0 MT | 24.35 | 34.05 | 1.40 | 24.3 | ≈ 0% |
| Maruti Swift 1.2 MT | 24.80 | 32.85 | 1.32 | 23.5 | −5% |
| Maruti Baleno 1.2 MT | 23.80 | 33.61 | 1.41 | 24.0 | +1% |
| Maruti Dzire 1.2 MT | 24.79 | 33.73 | 1.36 | 24.1 | −3% |
| Tata Punch 1.2 MT | 20.09 | 26.99 | 1.34 | 19.3 | −4% |
| Hyundai Exter 1.2 MT | 19.40 | 27.10 | 1.40 | 19.4 | ≈ 0% |

The pattern is remarkably consistent. Across six different engines and three different manufacturers, the CNG variant's petrol-equivalent efficiency lands within about **±5%** of the petrol variant. Some are a little better, some a little worse. None is 40% better. The "CNG gives more mileage" claim, taken at face value, is a unit conversion pretending to be an engineering breakthrough.

That does not make CNG a bad choice. It makes the *reason* for choosing it different from the reason most people give.

## So why does CNG sometimes genuinely improve efficiency?

The ±5% spread in that table is not noise. It is the sum of several real physical effects, some helping CNG and some hurting it. Understanding them tells you when CNG will be at its best and when it will disappoint.

### 1. Higher octane lets a dedicated engine compress harder

This is the biggest genuine efficiency lever, and the one most Indian CNG cars do not get to use.

Petrol's 91–95 octane rating exists to stop the fuel igniting before the spark plug fires. Compress an air-petrol mixture too far and it knocks, which can destroy pistons. CNG's octane rating is roughly 120–130, so a CNG engine can run a much higher compression ratio without knocking. A higher compression ratio means a higher expansion ratio: the hot gases push the piston down through a greater proportion of the cylinder's volume before the exhaust valve opens, so more of the fuel's heat is converted into useful work instead of leaving through the exhaust.

A purpose-built CNG engine can run 12:1 or even higher, against 10:1–11:1 for a typical petrol engine. Studies of dedicated CNG engines have found thermal-efficiency gains of roughly 5–10% from higher compression and optimised combustion, with the exact number depending on combustion-chamber design, spark timing and how far the engine is optimised for gas. Honda's dedicated Civic GX CNG engine, for example, used a higher compression ratio and a modified intake system specifically to recover the efficiency and power lost to gaseous fuelling.

But here is the catch for Indian buyers: **most CNG cars sold here are bi-fuel**. They have one engine that must run on both petrol and CNG, so the compression ratio is set by petrol's knock limit. A bolt-on sequential kit does not change the compression ratio, the combustion chamber or the piston. It simply adds gas injectors and a second fuel map. The high-octane advantage is therefore mostly left on the table. You get the cheaper fuel, but not the dedicated-CNG engine's full thermodynamic benefit.

This is why a factory CNG variant and a well-engineered dedicated CNG engine are not the same thing as "CNG is inherently more efficient." The fuel allows a better engine; most cars on Indian roads are not that engine.

### 2. A gas mixes better with air than a liquid does

Petrol is injected as a fine spray, but it is still a liquid. Some of it lands on the intake port walls and cylinder walls, especially when the engine is cold. That "wall wetting" is one reason a cold petrol engine needs extra fuel enrichment: the ECU has to compensate for the petrol that condenses instead of burning. In stop-start traffic, where the engine is constantly transitioning between load and idle, some fuel is inevitably wasted on wetting and re-evaporation.

CNG arrives as a gas. It mixes with air at the molecular level, so there is no liquid film to evaporate, no puddle in the intake, and no extra fuel needed to compensate for wall wetting. Combustion is more homogeneous, and transient fuelling is more precise. This can improve real-world efficiency in exactly the conditions where Indian cars spend most of their lives: cold starts, short trips and stop-start traffic.

The effect is real, but it is modest — typically a few percent, not tens of percent. And it is partly cancelled by the fact that many CNG cars start on petrol and switch to gas only after the engine warms up, so the coldest, least efficient part of the journey may still be on petrol.

### 3. The engine breathes through a wider throttle at part load

This one is subtle, and it is the most interesting positive effect.

A petrol engine controls power with a throttle plate. At light load — cruising slowly, idling, crawling through traffic — the plate is nearly closed, and the pistons have to work against a partial vacuum to pull air in. Engineers call this **pumping loss**. It is a real efficiency tax, and it is worst at exactly the low-load conditions of city driving.

A CNG engine has a problem that turns into a partial solution: the gas occupies volume in the intake charge, displacing some of the air. That reduces volumetric efficiency by roughly 4–10%, which is why CNG engines make less power. But to deliver the same road load, the ECU opens the throttle wider. A wider throttle means less intake vacuum, and therefore lower pumping losses. The engine is working less hard to breathe, which offsets part of the volumetric-efficiency penalty.

In a well-calibrated CNG engine, this can make part-load efficiency roughly equal to, or slightly better than, petrol — even though the engine makes less peak power. It is one reason CNG can feel surprisingly willing in gentle city driving and then feel flat when you ask for full throttle or climb a hill.

### 4. CNG avoids petrol's evaporative and dilution losses

Petrol is volatile. Your tank vents vapours, especially on hot days, and the refuelling process itself releases hydrocarbon vapour. Modern cars have evaporative-emission controls, but the losses are not zero. CNG is sealed in a high-pressure cylinder, so there is no evaporation and no refuelling vapour loss.

Petrol also dilutes engine oil over time, especially in cars that do a lot of short trips. Fuel that gets past the piston rings ends up in the sump, reducing the oil's lubricity and gradually increasing friction. CNG does not dilute oil in the same way, so a CNG engine's oil stays cleaner for longer. That does not show up on a brochure, but over 100,000 km it can help the engine hold its efficiency better than a petrol engine that has been short-tripped its whole life.

### 5. Cleaner combustion means less long-term efficiency decay

CNG contains no benzene, no aromatics and essentially no sulphur, and it burns with near-zero particulate matter. It leaves fewer deposits on intake valves, pistons and spark plugs than petrol. A clean combustion chamber holds its compression, its spark quality and its airflow better over time.

This is a long-term maintenance effect, not a showroom-number effect. A new CNG car and a new petrol car may be within a few percent of each other. Five years later, if the petrol car has been running rich, short-tripped and serviced late, while the CNG car has been maintained properly, the gap can widen in CNG's favour. But that is as much about maintenance as about the fuel.

## The other side: why CNG often loses efficiency

A fair answer has to include the effects that push the other way. They are the reason the table above shows −5% for some models rather than +10% across the board.

### Volumetric efficiency: the gas displaces air

This is the big one. In a spark-ignition engine, power is limited by how much air-fuel mixture you can get into the cylinder. CNG is already a gas, so it takes up space in the intake charge that would otherwise be filled with air. The result is a 4–10% reduction in volumetric efficiency, and a corresponding loss of power — typically 8–15% in a converted bi-fuel engine, depending on the kit and calibration.

Less power per cycle means the driver has to press the accelerator harder to achieve the same acceleration or climb the same hill. If the driver responds by driving more aggressively, the efficiency advantage of the gas disappears. This is why some CNG owners report excellent mileage and others report disappointing numbers from the same car: the fuel is the same, but the right foot is not.

### Slower flame speed

Methane's flame speed is lower than petrol's. In practical terms, the flame takes slightly longer to travel across the combustion chamber, so the engine needs more spark advance to burn the charge at the right moment. At low and medium speeds, a good calibration handles this easily. At high rpm and high load, the engine may not have enough time to complete combustion optimally, which can hurt efficiency and power.

This is one reason CNG's efficiency advantage tends to shrink — or turn into a disadvantage — on the highway, at high speeds and under heavy load. The city is CNG's comfort zone; a sustained 120 km/h cruise is not.

### The weight and packaging penalty

A CNG cylinder is a pressure vessel. A steel 55-litre water-capacity cylinder can weigh roughly 60–70 kg empty; a composite cylinder is lighter, but still adds meaningful mass. Add 8–10 kg of gas for a full fill, and you are carrying roughly 50–80 kg of extra mass in a small hatchback — about the weight of an adult passenger — on every trip, whether the tank is full or not.

Extra weight costs fuel in stop-start driving, where every acceleration has to be paid for. It also changes the car's balance and packaging: the cylinder usually sits in the boot, raising the centre of gravity and eating luggage space. None of this shows up in the km/kg number, but it is part of the real-world efficiency story.

### The bi-fuel compromise

A bi-fuel car has to run well on two fuels with very different properties. The compression ratio is a compromise, the combustion chamber is a compromise, and the ECU carries two fuel maps that cannot both be optimal. The CNG map is usually calibrated for emissions, driveability and safety, not for maximum thermal efficiency. A dedicated CNG engine can do better, but it cannot run on petrol, and it is rare in India.

### Gas quality and tank pressure

CNG is not a perfectly uniform fuel. Its methane content and calorific value vary by source and season. A kilogram of gas with a higher methane fraction carries more energy than a kilogram with more nitrogen and carbon dioxide. The ECU's fuel trims adapt, but they cannot change the fact that the fuel itself has less energy per kilogram on some days.

Tank pressure matters too. As the cylinder empties, the pressure drops, and the injection system has to work across a wide range. A car that feels strong on a full tank can feel slightly softer near empty. This does not necessarily change the measured km/kg much, but it changes the driving experience — and sometimes the driver's behaviour.

## City versus highway: where the mileage advantage actually lives

If you put all of these effects together, a pattern emerges. CNG's efficiency advantage is strongest at low and medium load, in warm conditions, with a well-calibrated engine. It is weakest at high load, high speed and low temperature. That is the opposite of the pattern many people expect.

| Driving condition | Typical CNG energy efficiency vs petrol | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| City stop-start, warm engine, gentle driving | Equal to +5% | Better mixing, lower pumping losses at part load, no cold-start enrichment |
| City stop-start, cold engine, short trips | −5% to −15% | Engine friction and warm-up dominate; many cars run on petrol until warm; extra weight |
| Steady 60–80 km/h | Equal to +3% | Both engines near their efficient zone; CNG's lower pumping loss helps |
| Steady 100–120 km/h | −5% to −10% | Volumetric-efficiency penalty and slower flame speed start to bite |
| Full load, uphill, or heavy luggage | −10% to −20% | CNG's lower volumetric efficiency and power deficit force harder throttle |
| Air-conditioning on, small engine | −10% to −20% (both fuels) | The compressor load is proportionally larger; CNG's power deficit makes it worse |
| Aggressive acceleration | −10% to −25% | The driver spends more time at high load, where CNG is weakest |
| Poorly calibrated venturi kit | −10% to −20% | Imprecise fuelling, backfires, poor transient response |
| Well-calibrated sequential kit | Equal to +5% | Precise gas injection and good ECU calibration recover most of the penalty |

These are directional estimates, not guarantees. The exact numbers depend on the car, the kit, the driver, the traffic and the weather. But the shape of the pattern is consistent across owner reports and test data: **CNG is a city fuel, not a highway fuel.** If your driving is mostly 100 km/h expressway runs, the efficiency case for CNG is weaker — though the price case may still be strong enough to justify it.

## The money math: cheaper per kilometre, not magically more efficient

Now put the efficiency question aside and look at the number that actually matters: cost per kilometre.

At the Delhi prices used across this site for October 2026 — petrol at ₹102.12 per litre and CNG at ₹86.98 per kilogram — the energy cost comparison looks like this:

| | Petrol | CNG |
|---|---|---|
| Price at the pump | ₹102.12 / litre | ₹86.98 / kg |
| Energy per unit | ~33.5 MJ / litre | ~48 MJ / kg |
| **Cost per megajoule** | **~₹3.05** | **~₹1.81** |
| Real-world mileage (typical hatchback) | ~18 km/l | ~24 km/kg |
| **Cost per kilometre** | **~₹5.67** | **~₹3.62** |
| Cost for 1,000 km a month | ₹5,670 | ₹3,620 |
| Cost for 15,000 km a year | ₹85,100 | ₹54,300 |
| **Annual saving** | — | **~₹30,800** |

The important line is **cost per megajoule**. CNG is not cheaper because its mileage number looks 33% bigger — per unit of energy it gives about the same kilometres. It is cheaper because a megajoule of CNG costs about 40% less than a megajoule of petrol. The price gap is the engine of the savings; the mileage number is mostly a labelling difference.

This also explains why the savings vary so much by city. In Delhi, CNG at ₹86.98/kg against petrol at ₹102.12/l gives a large gap. In a city where CNG is ₹100/kg and petrol is ₹95/l, the gap narrows or disappears. The mileage of your car matters, but the price ratio matters more. Always calculate with your own city's rates — [today's CNG price by city](/cng-price-in-india/) and [today's petrol price](/petrol-price-in-india/) — not with a national average.

### What if your CNG car is less efficient than the petrol one?

Suppose the CNG version is 5% less efficient in energy terms, as some of the models in the table are. Does that wipe out the saving? No. At 24 km/kg, the cost is ₹3.62/km. If the CNG engine were 5% less efficient than an energy-equivalent petrol engine, the "fair" CNG mileage would be about 25.3 km/kg, and the cost at that mileage would be ₹3.44/km. The actual cost at 24 km/kg is still ₹3.62/km — far below petrol's ₹5.67/km. A 5% efficiency penalty is easily absorbed by a 40% price advantage.

The reverse is also true: if CNG were priced the same per megajoule as petrol, the efficiency difference would decide the winner, and it would be a close fight. In India, it is not a close fight on cost, because the fuels are not priced on energy. They are priced on a mix of gas sourcing, taxes, city-gas distributor margins and politics, as I explain in [how CNG prices are calculated in India](/blog/how-cng-prices-are-calculated-in-india/).

### What the payback really depends on

The payback on a CNG kit or a factory CNG premium depends on three numbers:

1. **The price gap per kilometre** — your city's CNG price, your car's km/kg, and your petrol alternative's km/l.
2. **How many kilometres you drive a year** — the more you drive, the faster the fixed cost is recovered.
3. **How long you keep the car** — the savings only count if you own the car long enough to collect them.

At a saving of roughly ₹2 per kilometre, a ₹50,000 retrofit pays back in about 25,000 km — around 20 months for a 1,500 km/month driver, or four years for a 500 km/month driver. A ₹95,000 factory premium takes roughly 47,500 km. The full table, including the five-year ownership view, is in [petrol vs diesel vs CNG running costs](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/). The mileage number changes the payback by a few months; the price gap and your annual distance change it by years.

## How to measure your own CNG mileage properly

Most CNG mileage arguments are settled with bad data. The dashboard gauge is not a fuel gauge in the way you think it is: it reads cylinder pressure, not mass, and pressure falls non-linearly as the gas is used. A gauge that drops from full to half over the first 80 km does not mean you are getting 160 km on a tank. It means the pressure curve is doing what pressure curves do.

The reliable method is tank-to-tank, in kilograms:

**Step 1 — Fill at the same station and, if possible, the same dispenser.** Fill to the automatic cut-off. Note the kilograms added and the odometer reading. Do not top up after the cut-off; the cut-off is your reference point.

**Step 2 — Drive normally for 200–300 km.** Use your real mix of city, highway, AC and load. Do not hypermile for this test; you want the number you will actually live with.

**Step 3 — Refill at the same station and dispenser, to the same cut-off.** Note the kilograms added and the new odometer reading.

**Step 4 — Calculate.**

**km/kg = (new odometer − old odometer) ÷ kilograms added**

For example: 278 km driven, 11.6 kg refilled → 23.97 km/kg.

**Step 5 — Convert to petrol-equivalent if you want to compare engines.**

**Petrol-equivalent km/l = km/kg ÷ 1.4**

In the example: 23.97 ÷ 1.4 = **17.1 km/l**. That is the number to put next to a petrol car's km/l if you are comparing fuel efficiency rather than fuel cost.

**Step 6 — Calculate cost per kilometre.**

**Cost/km = price per kg ÷ km/kg**

At ₹86.98/kg and 23.97 km/kg: 86.98 ÷ 23.97 = **₹3.63/km**.

**Step 7 — Repeat three times and average.** A single tank is noisy: station pressure, temperature, traffic and AC use can all move the number by 5–10%. Three fills give you a trend you can trust.

If you are comparing your CNG car with a petrol car, do the same tank-to-tank test in the petrol car over the same routes, in the same week, with the same driver and the same AC setting. Do not compare a CNG city figure with a petrol highway figure, and do not compare either with the ARAI brochure. The lab cycle is a reference point, not a prediction.

## Why your CNG car returns less than the brochure

If your CNG car is delivering 20–24 km/kg in the city while the brochure says 33–35, you are not alone, and your car is probably not broken. ARAI figures are generated under controlled conditions: a prescribed speed cycle, no traffic, no AC, ideal tyre pressure, a test weight and a warmed-up engine. Real Indian driving adds traffic, heat, passengers, potholes and the occasional urgency. A 20–30% gap between the lab and the road is normal for both fuels.

Still, a sudden or unusually large drop is worth investigating. Here is a practical diagnostic table:

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden 10–20% drop in km/kg | Fuel quality, station pressure, a change in route or driving | Try a different high-turnover station; compare the last three fills; check tyre pressure |
| Gradual drop over months | Clogged CNG filter, dirty air filter, worn spark plugs, ageing O2 sensor | Service the CNG filter every 10,000–15,000 km; replace the air filter and plugs on schedule |
| Rough idle or hesitation on CNG | Kit calibration, spark plugs, ignition coil, vacuum leak | Get the kit checked by an authorised installer; scan for fault codes |
| Fine on petrol, weak on CNG | CNG filter, pressure regulator, gas injectors, low tank pressure | Refill at a high-pressure station; have the gas side inspected |
| Strong gas smell | Leak | Stop driving, ventilate, get it inspected immediately — do not treat this as a mileage issue |
| Gauge drops quickly but km/kg is normal | Pressure-based gauge behaviour | Ignore the gauge shape; trust the tank-to-tank calculation |
| Poor performance near empty | Low cylinder pressure | Refill earlier; avoid running the tank to the last kilometre |
| Mileage improves on the highway | Normal CNG behaviour | CNG is happiest at steady, moderate loads; city stop-start is harder on it |

One more variable that is easy to miss: **the CNG filter**. It is a service item, not a lifetime part. A clogged filter restricts gas flow, the ECU compensates, and efficiency drops. If your car has done 15,000 km since the last filter change, that is the first thing to check — before you blame the fuel or the engine.

## Myths and mistakes that refuse to die

**"CNG gives 30 km/kg, so it is 60% more efficient than petrol at 18 km/l."**
This is the unit trap. 30 km/kg is about 21.4 km/l petrol-equivalent. The real efficiency difference is small, and can go either way.

**"CNG has more energy per litre."**
It has more energy per kilogram, but far less per litre at 200 bar — about 9–10 MJ/L against petrol's 32–34.5 MJ/L. That is why the tank is big and heavy. Per kilogram, CNG is comparable to or slightly better than petrol; per litre of tank volume, it is much worse.

**"CNG burns hotter, so it gives more mileage."**
Flame temperature is not the main driver of efficiency. The compression ratio, the expansion ratio, the air-fuel mixture and the pumping losses are. CNG's flame temperature is broadly similar to petrol's; the efficiency difference comes from how the engine uses the charge, not from "hotter gas."

**"A CNG kit increases the compression ratio."**
No. A bolt-on kit adds a second fuel system. It does not change the pistons, the cylinder head or the compression ratio. Only a purpose-built CNG engine can exploit the fuel's high octane to raise compression.

**"CNG gives more power."**
No. A typical bi-fuel CNG conversion loses 8–15% of peak power because the gas displaces air in the intake. The car feels slower, especially when fully loaded or climbing. That is the trade-off for the lower running cost.

**"CNG damages the engine."**
Not when the kit is approved, installed by an authorised workshop and calibrated properly. Older engines without hardened valve seats can suffer valve-seat recession on dry gaseous fuel; modern engines are designed for it. The bigger risks are a cheap, poorly calibrated kit and skipped maintenance, not CNG itself.

**"You should run only on CNG to save the most."**
Running 100% on gas can let the petrol-side seals and injectors dry out over time. The usual advice is to run on petrol for roughly 10% of your kilometres, which costs very little and keeps both systems healthy.

**"A full CNG tank always holds the same amount."**
No. The mass of gas a cylinder holds depends on the station's pressure and the gas temperature. A "full" tank on a hot afternoon can hold less mass than a "full" tank on a cool morning. This affects range, not necessarily your km/kg, because the dispenser measures mass — but it is why two fills can cost different amounts.

**"CNG is metered in litres."**
In India, CNG is sold by the kilogram. If someone quotes you a price per litre of CNG, they are either converting from mass using a density assumption or they are confused. Ask for the kg rate.

**"CNG is always cheaper per kilometre."**
Almost always in India, but not universally. In a city with an unusually high CNG price and a highly efficient petrol car, the gap can be small. And if you drive very few kilometres, the upfront kit or variant premium can wipe out the fuel saving. Calculate your own numbers.

## When CNG genuinely wins — and when petrol does

CNG is the better choice, on both efficiency and cost, when:

- **You drive a lot.** The more kilometres you cover, the more the price gap compounds. For 15,000+ km a year, the case is strong.
- **Your driving is mostly city.** CNG's part-load efficiency and clean combustion shine in stop-start traffic, and the lower speed keeps its volumetric-efficiency weakness out of the picture.
- **You have easy access to a station.** A station on your daily route is worth more than a theoretical saving you cannot conveniently collect.
- **You keep cars for a long time.** The upfront cost needs time to pay back; a three-year ownership horizon may not be enough for a low-mileage driver.
- **You buy factory CNG.** You get the warranty, the calibration and the packaging sorted, and you avoid the retrofit paperwork and insurance questions.
- **You care about urban air quality.** CNG's near-zero particulate and lower CO2 per kilometre are real, as I explain in [why CNG is called a clean fuel](/blog/why-cng-is-called-clean-fuel/).

Petrol is the better choice when:

- **You drive under about 10,000–12,000 km a year.** The payback stretches beyond the point where many people sell the car.
- **You need boot space and long range.** A CNG cylinder eats luggage space, and a full tank typically gives 180–300 km against 500–600 km on petrol.
- **Your driving is highway-heavy.** CNG's efficiency advantage shrinks at high speed, and station density outside cities is still uneven.
- **You own a turbocharged direct-injection car.** These engines often cannot run purely on gas, and the conversion is expensive and technically fussy.
- **You value power and refinement.** CNG's 8–15% power loss is noticeable in a small engine with the AC on and a full load.
- **You are not prepared for the paperwork.** RC endorsement, insurance declaration, hydrotest every three years and a proper kit are not optional. An undocumented conversion is a safety and insurance problem, not a saving.

The distinction that matters here is between **efficiency** and **cost**. Petrol can win on efficiency in highway and high-load conditions while still losing on cost per kilometre. CNG can win on cost per kilometre while being slightly less efficient in energy terms. Decide which question you are actually asking.

## How to get the most kilometres out of a CNG car

The difference between 20 km/kg and 26 km/kg is rarely the car. It is usually the tyres, the traffic, the AC and the right foot. The practical levers:

- **Check tyre pressure every week or two.** Use the door-jamb placard, not the maximum pressure on the sidewall. A 2 psi drop can cost 1–2% in fuel.
- **Replace the air filter on schedule.** A clogged filter makes the engine work harder, and the effect is larger on a small CNG engine.
- **Replace the CNG filter every 10,000–15,000 km.** This is the most commonly skipped CNG-specific service item.
- **Use the right spark plugs and replace them on time.** CNG is harder on ignition components than petrol; a worn plug shows up as rough running and poor mileage.
- **Keep the kit calibrated.** A sequential kit that has drifted rich or lean will waste gas. Have it checked annually or if the behaviour changes.
- **Drive smoothly.** Gentle acceleration, early lifting, steady cruising. The CNG engine rewards smoothness because it has less torque to spare.
- **Use the AC intelligently.** Recirculate, park in shade, and avoid full-blast cooling on short trips. The compressor is a significant load on a small engine.
- **Remove unnecessary weight.** A full boot and a roof rack cost fuel on every trip. The CNG cylinder already adds enough weight.
- **Plan fills around high-turnover stations.** A station with fresh, high-pressure gas gives a better fill and consistent quality. Avoid filling at a station whose compressor is struggling.
- **Do not run the tank to empty.** Low pressure affects driveability and can make the last few kilometres inefficient. Refill at around a quarter tank.
- **Run 10% on petrol.** It keeps the petrol-side injectors, seals and pump exercised.
- **Keep the hydrotest and paperwork current.** A CNG car with an expired cylinder test is not a bargain; it is a liability. The rules are covered in [why CNG is better than petrol](/blog/why-cng-is-better-than-petrol/).
- **Use a fuel log.** Three tank-to-tank fills tell you more than a year of dashboard glances. If the number moves, you will see it early.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does CNG give more mileage than petrol?

Because CNG is sold by the kilogram and petrol by the litre, and a kilogram of CNG contains roughly 1.35–1.45 times the energy of a litre of petrol. When you convert CNG's km/kg to a petrol-equivalent km/l, the apparent 70–80% advantage shrinks to roughly ±5%. CNG's engine is not twice as efficient; the unit is simply larger. The real financial advantage is that CNG costs less per megajoule than petrol.

### Is CNG actually more fuel-efficient than petrol?

In energy terms, usually about the same. Across six popular Indian models, the CNG variant's petrol-equivalent efficiency was within about ±5% of the petrol variant's. A purpose-built CNG engine with a high compression ratio can be a few percent more efficient; a bi-fuel conversion can be a few percent less. The cost per kilometre is much lower, but that is mostly a price effect, not an efficiency effect.

### How do I convert CNG km/kg to petrol km/l?

Divide the CNG figure by about 1.4. For example, 28 km/kg ÷ 1.4 = 20 km/l petrol-equivalent. The exact factor depends on the energy content of the gas and the petrol, so treat 1.4 as a good approximation, not a precise constant. If you want to be more precise, use the calorific values from your gas distributor and the density of your petrol.

### How much mileage does a CNG car give in India?

It varies widely. A small hatchback might return 22–26 km/kg in city driving and 27–31 km/kg on the highway; a larger sedan or SUV might return 18–24 km/kg in the city. The ARAI figures are higher — often 27–36 km/kg — because they are measured in a lab cycle. Use owner reports for your specific model, and do a tank-to-tank test for your own car.

### Why is my CNG car's mileage lower than the ARAI figure?

Because the ARAI cycle is not real driving. It has no traffic, no AC, no passengers, no hills and no aggressive acceleration. Real-world mileage is typically 15–30% lower for both petrol and CNG cars. If your gap is larger than that, check tyre pressure, the CNG filter, the air filter, spark plugs, kit calibration and your driving pattern before assuming something is wrong with the car.

### Does CNG reduce engine power?

Yes, typically by 8–15% in a bi-fuel conversion, because the gas displaces air in the intake and lowers volumetric efficiency. A dedicated CNG engine can recover some of that with a higher compression ratio and optimised intake design, but it will still make less peak power than a comparable petrol engine. In normal city driving you may not notice; fully loaded, uphill or with the AC on, you will.

### Is CNG good for highway driving?

It is cheaper, but it is not at its most efficient. At sustained high speed and high load, CNG's volumetric-efficiency penalty and slower flame speed reduce its advantage, and the smaller range means more stops. If your driving is mostly highway, a petrol or diesel car may be the better efficiency choice — though CNG can still be cheaper per kilometre if the price gap is large enough.

### Does CNG damage the engine?

Not when the installation is approved, calibrated and maintained. Modern engines have hardened valve seats and are designed to tolerate gaseous fuel. Older engines may need attention to valve-seat wear, and any engine will suffer from a cheap, poorly calibrated kit or skipped oil changes. The real risks are bad installations and bad maintenance, not CNG itself.

### Is CNG cheaper than petrol per kilometre in 2026?

In most Indian cities, yes, often by 30–40% at current prices. But it depends on your city's CNG rate, your car's km/kg, your petrol alternative's km/l and the upfront cost of the kit or variant. Calculate cost per kilometre with your own numbers rather than relying on a national average. The live rates are on our [CNG price page](/cng-price-in-india/) and [petrol price page](/petrol-price-in-india/).

### Can I install CNG in an automatic car?

Yes, but the options are narrower. Several manufacturers now offer factory CNG with AMT, and the aftermarket can convert some automatics, but the calibration is more complex and the warranty implications are bigger. The trade-offs are covered in [why CNG cars are not automatic](/blog/why-cng-cars-are-not-automatic/) — the short version is that a factory CNG automatic is now a real option, but a retrofit on a modern automatic needs a very good installer.

## Final thoughts

The question "why does CNG give more mileage than petrol?" has a satisfying answer, but it is not the answer most people expect. CNG's number is higher because a kilogram of gas carries more energy than a litre of liquid — about 1.4 litres of petrol worth. Once you normalise for that, the engine efficiency gap is small, sometimes positive and sometimes negative. The CNG engine's real advantages are a higher octane ceiling it rarely gets to use in bi-fuel form, cleaner and more homogeneous combustion, and lower pumping losses at part load. Its real disadvantages are lower volumetric efficiency, slower flame speed, extra weight and the compromises of a dual-fuel calibration.

What makes CNG worth buying in India is not the mileage number. It is the price per megajoule. At current Delhi prices, CNG is roughly 40% cheaper per unit of energy than petrol, and that gap is large enough to absorb a few percent of efficiency loss and still leave a substantial saving per kilometre. The mileage conversation is interesting; the price conversation is the one that pays.

So the next time someone tells you their CNG car does "32" while their petrol car did "18", ask them the question that settles it: **32 what, per what?** If the answer is km/kg versus km/l, you are looking at a unit conversion, not a miracle. Convert both to the same scale, and then decide with the numbers that actually matter — your city's prices, your real km/kg, your annual distance and how long you plan to keep the car.
