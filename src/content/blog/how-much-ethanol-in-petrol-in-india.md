A friend in Chandigarh pulled into a pump last month, watched the attendant key in "E20" on the dispenser, and texted me: "Is this safe for my 2015 Creta? And why does it say E20 when my friend in Jaipur says his pump still sells normal petrol?"

That question — how much ethanol is actually in Indian petrol, and does it vary — is one of the most common ones I get, and the honest answer is messier than a single percentage. It depends on where you live, which oil company's pump you use, and when you filled up. India has been moving from E10 (10% ethanol) toward E20 (20% ethanol) for years, and right now both blends are sitting side by side at pumps across the country.

Below is everything worth knowing about the ethanol percentage in Indian petrol, without the panic and without the marketing gloss — including how to check what you're actually buying, what it does to your mileage, and whether your vehicle can take it.

## The short answer

As of 2026, most petrol sold in India contains either **10% ethanol (E10)** or **20% ethanol (E20)**:

- **E10** — 90% petrol, 10% ethanol. This was the blend India standardised on for years, and it's still what many pumps outside the metros dispense.
- **E20** — 80% petrol, 20% ethanol. This is the newer, government-mandated grade now being rolled out nationwide, and it's already the default at a large share of pumps in most metro cities and state capitals.

There is no single national percentage you can quote and be right everywhere. The number on the dispenser at your local pump is what matters, and that number can change from city to city, brand to brand, and even month to month as oil marketing companies (IOCL, HPCL, BPCL) convert more dispensers.

## What E10 and E20 actually mean

Strip away the acronym and the blend is simple: the "E" stands for ethanol, and the number is the percentage **by volume**.

| Grade | Petrol | Ethanol | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal/unblended (legacy) | 100% | 0% | Plain petrol; increasingly rare on the formal network |
| E10 | 90% | 10% | The blend India phased in through the 2000s and 2010s |
| E20 | 80% | 20% | The next rung, now the mandated rollout target |
| E85 | 15% | 85% | Flex-fuel blend used in purpose-built vehicles (not standard Indian retail petrol) |

The ethanol itself is fuel-grade, denatured alcohol made by sugar mills and distilleries, drawn from a wide menu: C-heavy molasses, B-heavy molasses, damaged food grains, maize, cotton stubble, and increasingly cellulosic sources. The point of that menu is policy as much as chemistry — no single crop should end up controlling either fuel or food prices.

Why cap it at 20% for ordinary cars? Because roughly **20% is the ceiling at which standard petrol-engine fuel systems handle ethanol without material changes.** Ethanol is a solvent and it attracts water (hygroscopic). Below about 20%, the fuel stays compatible with the rubber seals, fuel lines, injectors, and carburettor parts fitted to modern engines. Above it — E85 territory — you need purpose-built flex-fuel hardware.

Two chemical properties of ethanol do the heavy lifting:

- **Octane.** Pure petrol sold in India is around 91 octane. Ethanol's research octane number is around **108**. Blending 20% of it lifts the effective octane into the mid-90s, which is a genuine mild upgrade for modern BS-VI engines with higher compression ratios and turbocharging.
- **Oxygen content.** Ethanol molecules carry oxygen into the combustion chamber, helping the fuel burn more completely. That's the source of E20's modest but real emissions advantage over pure petrol.

## Where India stands on the blending roadmap

India's Ethanol Blended Petrol (EBP) programme had a 5% target back in 2003 and a 10% target for 2022. The 10% target was effectively met in early 2023, and the government launched the E20 rollout on **5 February 2023**, ahead of schedule. The Cabinet-approved roadmap accelerated full E20 introduction across the country to **2025–26**, originally pencilled in for 2030.

So the programme is now firmly in the E20 phase, but "full introduction" means the infrastructure target, not that every single pump in every village dispenses nothing but E20 today. The reality on the ground:

- **Metro cities and most state capitals:** a large share — in many areas effectively all — of retail outlets dispense E20.
- **Semi-urban and rural areas:** a patchwork in transition. You'll still find E10 pumps, and sometimes both grades at the same company's different outlets.
- **The blend can vary by oil company and by month** as conversion of dispensers continues.

This is exactly why my friend in Chandigarh saw E20 while his friend in Jaipur saw "normal petrol" — different cities are at different points in the rollout, and both grades are legitimate, government-notified fuel.

## How to check what blend you're buying

You don't have to guess. Every converted dispenser is labelled, and every receipt lists the grade.

**At the pump:**
- Look for a green **"E20"** sticker or badge on the dispenser.
- The dispenser's printed grade marking will say E20 (or E10).
- Your printed receipt lists the grade dispensed.

**If you're unsure:**
- Ask the attendant — "E20 hai?" takes two words.
- Your oil marketing company's app (Indian Oil, HP, BPCL) often shows pump-level grades for your pincode.
- This site's [petrol price by city page](/petrol-price-in-india/) tracks the posted rates that result from the current blend in your city.

**One practical point that reassures most people:** E10 and E20 mix freely in your tank. There is no "contamination" from half a tank of one blend meeting a fill of the other. You do not need to run your tank to empty before switching grades, and you don't need to panic when a road trip takes you to a pump still on the older blend. The fuels are miscible and engines tolerate the in-between blend — this has been true since E10 arrived, and it remains true now.

## What the blend does to your mileage and bill

This is the part everyone actually wants to know, and it's the calculation nobody does at the pump. Ethanol contains less energy per litre than petrol — roughly **21 MJ per litre against petrol's ~33–34 MJ per litre.** Blend 20% of the weaker fuel into the mix and the fuel in your tank carries roughly **6–7% less chemical energy per litre** than the petrol it replaced. Real-world mileage loss lands at roughly **3–6% for most modern engines**, because the higher octane lets engine management extract slightly better combustion efficiency, partially offsetting the energy gap.

But a cheaper litre is not automatically a cheaper kilometre. Let's run the numbers properly, using **Delhi rates as of late October 2026** — normal petrol at ₹102.12 per litre — and an illustrative E20 price ₹6 lower, at ₹96.52:

**Cost per km = price per litre ÷ real-world km per litre**

Assume a typical hatchback doing an honest **18 km/l on normal petrol** (not the ARAI brochure figure — the real one). On E20, the same car typically returns **17.0–17.5 km/l**:

| | Normal petrol | E20 (₹6 cheaper, −3% mileage) | E20 (₹6 cheaper, −6% mileage) | E20 (same price, −6% mileage) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | ₹102.12 / litre | ₹96.52 / litre | ₹96.52 / litre | ₹102.12 / litre |
| Mileage | 18.0 km / litre | 17.5 km / litre | 16.9 km / litre | 16.9 km / litre |
| **Cost per kilometre** | **₹5.67** | **₹5.52** | **₹5.71** | **₹6.04** |
| 15,000 km a year | ₹85,100 | ₹82,800 | ₹85,700 | ₹90,600 |

Read that table honestly, because it cuts against the hype in both directions:

- **At a ₹6 discount and a modest mileage dip, E20 genuinely saves you money — roughly ₹2,300 a year at 15,000 km.**
- **At the same posted price with a 6% mileage dip, you'd actually pay more per kilometre — about ₹5,500 a year more.** That's exactly why the price gap on the board matters as much as the blend percentage.

E20's price is set the same way all petrol is set — base price, central excise duty, dealer commission, and state VAT stacked on top, exactly as I've broken down in [how fuel excise duty and state VAT are actually calculated](/blog/how-fuel-excise-duty-and-state-vat-are-calculated/), and revised daily on the same 6 AM mechanism described in [why petrol prices change at 6 AM](/blog/why-petrol-prices-change-at-6am-and-how-omcs-calculate-them/). Ethanol has, in most recent years, been cheaper per litre than petrol at the refinery gate, and oil marketing companies have passed a share of that through — commonly **₹3–8 a litre cheaper than normal petrol in most states**, with a few markets near parity.

For a bike doing 45 km/l, the same arithmetic applies — the percentages are identical, just on a smaller absolute bill.

## Can your car or bike take E20? The compatibility check

Let's take the scare headlines one at a time and replace each with the actual engineering.

**"E20 reduces mileage."** True — and now you know why and by how much. Expect 3–6% lower mileage on most modern engines. That's a small, predictable arithmetic dip, not a breakdown.

**"E20 makes less power."** At the crank, the difference is a rounding error for naturally aspirated engines — under 2–3% in most tests, often unnoticeable. Turbocharged and high-compression engines can actually benefit slightly from the octane bump. E20 will neither make your car faster nor neuter it.

**"Ethanol eats engine parts."** Ethanol is a solvent and is hygroscopic, so it can attack certain **older rubber and elastomer fuel-line formulations**, and it can attract water into a tank that sits half-empty for months. That's a real concern for a vehicle with thirty-year-old fuel hoses. It is not a concern for a BS6 car whose fuel system was specified for ethanol-blended fuel from the factory — which is most petrol cars sold in India since 2020, and many BS4 cars before them, because Indian petrol has contained some ethanol for years already. Your car has not been running pure petrol; it has been running E10 or thereabouts through much of its life. Going to E20 is a step up the same ladder, not a leap off a cliff.

**"It causes carbon buildup."** The evidence points the other way. Ethanol's solvent properties help keep intake valves and injectors cleaner than the heavier aromatic compounds in pure petrol.

**"Two-stroke bikes will die."** This is the one machine class where the caution is genuine. Two-stroke engines premix or meter oil into the combustion chamber, and ethanol's behaviour plays badly with their fuel-system materials and with oil-fuel mixtures left standing. If you run a two-stroke, **check with the manufacturer before switching**, and don't leave E20 sitting in the tank for months.

### Step-by-step compatibility check for your own vehicle

**Step 1 — Find your vehicle's build year and emission stage:**

| Your vehicle | E20 verdict |
|---|---|
| Flex-fuel / E85-capable vehicle | Fully designed for ethanol — E20 is its mildest fuel |
| BS6 petrol, roughly 2020 onwards (most modern cars) | Factory-compatible; your manual almost certainly lists E20 or E10 |
| Two-wheeler marketed as E20-compliant (most models from ~2023) | Fully compatible |
| BS4 petrol, roughly 2010–2020 | Generally fine — Indian BS4 fuel systems have long handled E10; check your manual for the E20 line |
| Older petrol vehicle, pre-2010, original fuel hoses and seals | Proceed with caution — inspect and ideally replace aged rubber fuel lines first |
| Two-stroke engine | Manufacturer check first; usually discouraged |
| Diesel vehicle of any age | Never — E20 is petrol; a diesel fuel system will not tolerate it |
| Classic / occasionally-run vehicle left standing for months | Fuel stability matters more than blend — fill it up, consider a stabiliser, run it occasionally |

**Step 2 — Read your owner's manual's fuel section.** Manufacturers publish explicit language like "ethanol-blended fuel up to 20% (E20) may be used" on modern cars. If your manual says E20, the compatibility question is closed — no forum opinion outranks the factory.

**Step 3 — If the manual is silent or old, ask the manufacturer, not the internet.** A two-minute call to the authorised helpline resolves it. The manufacturer has already tested your exact fuel-system materials against the blend.

**Step 4 — On older vehicles, inspect the fuel system before the first E20 tank.** Cracked, hardened or weeping fuel hoses, a corroded fuel-pump strainer, and a grimy carburettor bowl are pre-existing problems that ethanol can aggravate. Replacing aged rubber fuel lines costs a few thousand rupees — trivial next to the fuel-pump failure people are fearing.

**Step 5 — Don't fear mixing grades.** Half a tank of E10 topped up with E20 is chemically unremarkable.

If mileage has already dropped unexpectedly on your bike on any grade, the cause is more often a clogged injector, a fouled plug, or a choked air filter than the fuel itself. I've written the full diagnostic walkthrough in [why my motorcycle's mileage dropped](/blog/why-my-motorcycle-mileage-dropped-fuel-pump-tests/), and a ₹300 pump-side check will tell you which it is before you blame the ethanol.

## Why the government is pushing the blend so hard

E20 is not an environmental fashion statement. It's a three-birds-one-stone energy policy:

**One: crude oil imports.** India imports roughly **85–88% of the crude oil it refines.** Every litre of domestically produced ethanol that displaces a litre of petrol is a litre that doesn't have to be imported, shipped, and refined — foreign exchange that stays in the country. The Ministry of Petroleum has cited annual savings on the order of tens of thousands of crores of rupees from full-rate blending.

**Two: farmer income.** The ethanol procurement price is set by government formulas — the Fair and Remunerative Price on cane-based ethanol, and the Marginal Cost of Production plus 10% on grain-based ethanol. That gives sugar mills and distilleries a guaranteed buyer for molasses and surplus grain, which matters most in years when sugar or cereal prices crash. The cotton-stubble and cellulosic strands additionally give Punjab and Haryana's stubble-burning problem an economic alternative.

**Three: emissions.** Ethanol is an oxygenate — it burns cleaner than the aromatics it displaces. Government and industry assessments point to meaningful reductions in carbon monoxide, unburnt hydrocarbons, and particulate matter from ethanol-blended petrol, along with lower lifecycle CO2 than pure petrol, though the exact reduction depends on the feedstock. Nobody serious claims E20 is a climate solution on its own. It is a cheap, available, immediately deployable improvement across a fleet of hundreds of millions of engines.

## Common myths about ethanol-blended petrol, busted

- **"E20 is adulteration."** It is the opposite: a notified, government-mandated fuel grade with a published specification, dispensed through the formal oil marketing network. Adulteration is undeclared contamination; E20 is a labelled product.
- **"It's only for flex-fuel cars."** Flex-fuel cars run E85 *and* everything milder. Ordinary modern petrol cars run E20 by design; the compatibility chart above is the test, not the badge on a flex-fuel door.
- **"My mileage will collapse."** It will dip 3–6% — real, but bounded, and offset if your state prices E20 lower. Collapse is not on the menu.
- **"E20 will void my warranty."** Using a government-notified fuel grade for which the manufacturer declares compatibility is not a warranty event. If your vehicle predates the E20 language, older vehicles should take the caution seriously and inspect the fuel system first.
- **"The ethanol will phase-separate in my tank overnight."** Phase separation requires significant water contamination plus time — it is a storage problem, not a fill-and-drive problem. Normal weekly driving on a sealed modern fuel system does not produce it.
- **"Why bother with E20 when EVs are coming?"** Because the average Indian vehicle on the road today will still be petrol-powered well into the next decade, and E20 improves it overnight at zero purchase cost.

## Final thoughts

So how much ethanol is in petrol in India? Right now, it's **either 10% or 20%** — E10 or E20 — depending on your city, your pump, and your oil company. The country is moving from E10 to E20, both grades are legitimate government-notified fuels, and they mix freely in your tank so you don't need to drain anything when switching.

For you as a driver, the whole story reduces to three lines:

1. **Modern vehicle (BS6 car from ~2020, or E20-compliant two-wheeler from ~2023):** fill without a second thought. Check whether your state prices E20 lower — if so, you'll likely save a little per kilometre despite the small mileage dip.
2. **Older vehicle (pre-2010, original fuel hoses):** check the manual, inspect the hoses, and take the manufacturer's word over a WhatsApp forward.
3. **Everyone:** expect roughly 3–6% less mileage, do the per-kilometre math with your own board price, and refuse to be panicked by either the boosters or the doomsayers.

The blend in your pump is changing whether we argue about it or not. Understanding what's actually in the tank — and what it does to your specific machine and your specific bill — is the only part that was ever worth the argument.
