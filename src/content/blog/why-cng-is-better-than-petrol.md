My neighbour bought a CNG WagonR last year and, for about two months, I gave him a hard time about it every chance I got — boot space gone, power gone, "you'll spend your weekends queueing at the pump," I said. Then last Sunday he showed me his fuel app: 14,200 km on gas, and his entire year's fuel bill was roughly ₹31,000 lower than what I spent covering the same distance in my petrol hatchback.

So I did what I always do — sat down with current prices, kit costs, insurance changes and maintenance intervals, and tried to break his claim. I couldn't. For most city drivers in India, CNG really is better than petrol. Not for everyone, and not for every car — but on the numbers, it wins more often than the brochure debate suggests.

Here's the full case, with the actual math and the honest trade-offs.

**The short answer:** at today's Delhi prices, CNG costs roughly ₹3.6–4.0 per kilometre against petrol's ₹5.67 — about ₹2 saved on every single kilometre. Over a typical 15,000 km year, that's ₹26,000–₹31,000 back in your pocket before deducting a rupee of kit or maintenance cost. Add in roughly 25% lower CO2 per kilometre, near-zero particulate and sulphur emissions, and an infrastructure that grew from 738 stations to nearly 9,000 in a decade, and the argument gets one-sided pretty fast — right up until the point where it doesn't, which I'll get to at the end.

## The cost-per-kilometre math nobody does at the pump

Comparing ₹102 a litre of petrol with ₹87 a kilogram of CNG is meaningless, because a litre and a kilogram are different units carrying different energy and returning different mileage. The only comparison that matters is **cost per kilometre**:

**Cost per km = fuel price ÷ real-world mileage (km per litre or per kg)**

Using Delhi rates for 12 October 2026 — petrol at ₹102.12 per litre, CNG at ₹86.98 per kilogram — and honest owner-reported mileage for a typical hatchback (not the ARAI lab figure):

| | Petrol | CNG |
|---|---|---|
| Price | ₹102.12 / litre | ₹86.98 / kg |
| Real-world mileage | ~18 km / litre | ~22–24 km / kg |
| **Cost per kilometre** | **₹5.67** | **₹3.62 – ₹3.95** |
| 1,000 km every month | ₹5,670 | ₹3,620 – ₹3,950 |
| 15,000 km every year | ₹85,100 | ₹54,300 – ₹59,300 |

That's a saving of **₹1,720–₹2,050 a month**, or roughly **₹26,000–₹31,000 a year**, for driving exactly the way you already drive. You don't change your route or your habits — the fuel economics do the work.

And this isn't a Delhi-only trick. CNG varies wildly by city — ₹88 in Mumbai, ₹93.50 in Kolkata, ₹94 in Ahmedabad, ₹98 in Bengaluru — but petrol varies too. Even in Bengaluru, where CNG is among the priciest in the country, you're looking at around ₹4.45 per kilometre on gas against roughly ₹6.16 on petrol. The gap survives even at the expensive end. ## Why petrol is structurally the more expensive fuel

The per-kilometre gap isn't an accident or a temporary quirk — it's built into how the two fuels are priced.

Petrol in India carries heavy taxation on top of the crude oil itself: central excise duty as a fixed rupee amount per litre, plus state VAT that compounds on an already-taxed subtotal. In most states taxes account for around half of what you pay at the counter. I've broken that stack layer by layer in [how fuel excise duty and state VAT are actually calculated](/blog/how-fuel-excise-duty-and-state-vat-are-calculated/), and once you see it, the ₹102 makes sense.

CNG is not tax-free — it sits outside GST too, and it also pays excise plus state VAT. But it starts from a genuinely cheaper raw material: natural gas rather than refined crude, with a large share coming from cheaper domestic supply. The result is a lower base price that the taxes work on. The full mechanism — including why your city's CNG price can jump without petrol moving an inch — is covered in [how CNG prices are calculated in India](/blog/how-cng-prices-are-calculated-in-india/).

The practical takeaway: **the price advantage of CNG over petrol is structural, not promotional.** It has held through crude spikes, rupee falls and every excise revision of the past decade.

## The one-time cost: kit, paperwork, and when it pays back

Now the part everyone hand-waves — switching to CNG isn't free.

**Buying a factory-fitted CNG variant of a new car** typically costs ₹90,000–₹1,00,000 more than the petrol trim. That's a real premium, and it's why my earlier breakdown of [petrol vs diesel vs CNG running costs](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/) keeps warning people about breakeven distance.

**Converting a petrol car you already own** is cheaper. A proper BS6-compliant sequential MPI kit supplied and fitted runs ₹40,000–₹60,000. On top of that:

- **RTO endorsement** (recording the alteration on your RC): ₹1,000–₹3,000
- **Insurance endorsement** (adding the kit to your policy): ₹1,500–₹3,500, one time
- **Total day-one cost: roughly ₹42,500–₹66,500**, depending on car and city

So how long until that pays back? At a conservative ₹2 per kilometre saved:

| You drive | Retrofit pays back in | Factory premium (~₹95k) |
|---|---|---|
| 12,000 km / year | ~2.3 years (27,500 km) | ~4 years (47,500 km) |
| 18,000 km / year | ~1.5 years | ~2.6 years |
| 30,000 km / year | under 11 months | ~1.6 years |

Run it out to five years at 15,000 km a year: gross fuel saving of about ₹1.53 lakh, minus roughly ₹15,000 in the extra ownership costs listed below, minus the ₹55,000 retrofit — you're **ahead by around ₹84,000**. Do the same math on a ₹95,000 factory premium and you're still ahead by roughly ₹44,000. Either way, after breakeven, every kilometre is close to pure saving.

The honest caveat from that same running-costs analysis still applies: **the breakeven distance has to fit your ownership horizon.** If you sell cars every three years and drive 10,000 km annually, the math is marginal. If you drive 1,500 km a month or more — which describes most city commuters, cab drivers and delivery fleets — CNG pays for itself while you're still getting to know the car.

## What it actually does to your car (the honest version)

A good CNG conversion doesn't ruin a car, but it does change it. Knowing the real list up front beats discovering it later:

- **Power:** expect a modest dip, typically in the 5–10% range on a properly tuned sequential kit — noticeable mainly when overtaking fully loaded or climbing a gradient. (Older venturi-era conversions tested far worse; that's one of several reasons to avoid them.)
- **Mileage reading:** distance per kilogram is close to, sometimes slightly above, your petrol litres — 18 km/l often becomes 22–24 km/kg. The saving comes from the price of the unit, not from magic efficiency. I've broken down the km/kg versus km/l conversion, and where the real efficiency difference comes from, in [why CNG gives more mileage than petrol](/blog/why-cng-gives-more-mileage-than-petrol/).
- **Boot space:** a retrofit cylinder occupies a meaningful chunk of it. Factory-fitted cars have got cleverer — under-floor tanks and twin-cylinder designs give much of the boot back — but a retrofit on an older car will not.
- **Running costs go up slightly:** once the kit is declared, your insurance own-damage premium typically rises 10–15%, the cylinder needs hydrotesting every three years at around ₹3,000, and the CNG filter gets replaced every 10,000–15,000 km for under ₹1,000. Call it ₹2,500–₹4,000 a year — real money, but a fraction of the ₹26,000+ fuel saving.
- **One habit worth keeping:** run the car on petrol for roughly 10% of your driving and stick to your normal service schedule. It keeps the petrol-side fuel system and seals exercised — which is also the entire basis of the "CNG dries out the engine" myth.

## The emissions case is stronger than most people think

"CNG is cleaner" gets thrown around so often it has stopped meaning anything. Here's what the numbers actually say:

| Measure | Petrol | CNG |
|---|---|---|
| CO2 intensity, average four-wheeler | ~190 g / km | ~143 g / km — about **25% less** |
| Carbon monoxide in controlled tests | baseline | up to ~70% lower |
| Benzene and aromatics in the fuel | present | none |
| Sulphur and particulate matter | present | near-zero |

Independent assessments of Indian four-wheelers put average intensity at around 190 grams of CO2 per kilometre for petrol against 143 grams for CNG; European chassis-dynamometer and on-road studies have found lab reductions near 23% and real-world reductions approaching 30%. CNG is methane — it contains none of the benzene and aromatic compounds petrol carries into city air, and it burns with essentially no particulate matter.

Why that matters more here than somewhere else: road transport is a dominant source of urban pollution in Indian metros, and the cities that went furthest with gas did it for exactly this reason. Delhi runs its bus fleet, its taxis and its auto-rickshaws on CNG because converting public fleets was the single biggest clean-air lever the city ever pulled. A private car doing the same thing per kilometre isn't a marketing line — it's the same lever at a smaller scale.

## "But there are no CNG stations" — a 2015 objection

If your mental image of CNG is one queue and a leaking nozzle, it's a decade out of date.

According to the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board's own data (pngrb.gov.in), India had **8,916 CNG stations as of 31 March 2026**, up from 6,861 two years earlier and just **738 in 2014** — a twelve-fold expansion in twelve years, with authorised city-gas networks now covering 99% of the population. The regulator's target is **18,336 stations by 2030**, with highway corridors explicitly part of the plan.

Uttar Pradesh alone has 1,367 stations, Maharashtra 1,309, Gujarat 1,102, Haryana 603. The station map is still uneven — that part of the old objection survives — which is exactly why step one of any CNG decision is checking stations near *your* home and *your* office, not near mine. Fifteen minutes on a map beats any national statistic.

## Is it safe? What the rules actually require

The scary image of CNG comes from illegal, unendorsed roadside fitments — and those genuinely are dangerous. What the legal route looks like is a different story:

- **Approved kits, approved hands.** Central Motor Vehicles Rules (rule 115-B) place type approval on the kit manufacturer and require fitting by workshops authorised by them, tested to the AIS-024 safety standard. Reputable kits carry CCOE approval, and the cylinder comes with a PESO certificate.
- **Mandatory testing.** Every cylinder must be hydrotested every three years (around ₹3,000) — the test that catches the fatigue the scary videos are actually about.
- **Built-in safeguards.** Cylinders carry pressure relief devices, the system cuts off automatically, and CNG is lighter than air, so a leak disperses upward instead of pooling under the car the way LPG does.
- **Paperwork that protects you.** The alteration must be endorsed on your RC (report it within 14 days; the fee works out around ₹1,000–₹3,000 with state levies) and declared to your insurer. Skip this and a gas-related claim can be rejected outright — which is the real danger in most "CNG accident" stories.

In short: a legal, endorsed, periodically tested installation is a genuinely safe system. An undocumented ₹30,000 roadside fitment is not — and no amount of "CNG is dangerous" articles change which one people are actually reacting to.

## Where petrol still beats CNG (yes, really)

A post that only argued one side would be an ad, not an article. Petrol genuinely wins in these situations:

- **Low annual mileage.** Under 10,000–12,000 km a year, the breakeven stretches past the point most people keep their cars. The fuel saving never catches the upfront cost.
- **Boot space and range.** A retrofit cylinder eats a serious share of the boot, and a full CNG tank typically covers roughly 180–250 km against 500–600 km on a full petrol tank. If your life is airport runs with two suitcases, that's not a rounding error — it's every trip.
- **Turbo and direct-injection engines.** Direct-injection petrol engines can't run purely on gas — the injectors stay lubricated by a petrol fraction, so the ratio caps around 80:20, and the kits cost ₹1–1.5 lakh. The savings math stretches accordingly.
- **Factory CNG is still mostly manual-only.** Several brands, Maruti included, offer their factory CNG variants primarily with manual transmissions. Want a torque-converter automatic on gas? An aftermarket conversion is often your only route — with the warranty questions that come with it.
- **Highway-heavy lives.** Station density on intercity routes is improving fast but is still nothing like the petrol pump network. If most of your kilometres are outside your city, petrol remains the simpler fuel.
- **Warranty peace of mind on a brand-new car.** Fitting an aftermarket kit to a car still under factory warranty is a grey area most manufacturers won't cover powertrain claims through. A factory-fitted variant sidesteps this entirely; a retrofit on a car with years of warranty left does not.

## How to decide for your own car: a six-step check

**Step 1** — Look up today's CNG and petrol prices for your city ([CNG rates here](/cng-price-in-india/), petrol rates on your oil company's app) and calculate both cost-per-km figures with the formula above.

**Step 2** — Count CNG stations within about 10 minutes of your home and your workplace. One station you'll actually pass daily beats five you'll never visit.

**Step 3** — Be honest about annual distance. If the odometer is under 12,000 km a year, stop here — petrol is probably the better total-cost choice, as covered in the [running-costs comparison](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/).

**Step 4** — Decide factory or retrofit. New car → factory-fitted CNG variant if it exists in the variant you want. Already own a petrol car with years left on it → retrofit, ideally once the factory warranty has expired.

**Step 5** — Do the paperwork before you celebrate: RC endorsement, insurance endorsement, CCOE-approved kit from an authorised installer, PESO cylinder certificate, diary entry for the three-year hydrotest.

**Step 6** — Plug your own numbers into the payback table: (kit cost ÷ your per-km saving) ÷ your annual km = years to breakeven. If it fits comfortably inside how long you'll keep the car, switch. If it doesn't, you've just saved yourself ₹50,000.

## Common mistakes people make with CNG

- **Buying on kit price alone.** The installer matters more than the brand sticker. A mediocre install of an excellent kit drives worse than a careful install of an average one — ask for references, not just a quotation.
- **Choosing a venturi (open-loop) kit to save money.** They're outdated, less efficient, and a poor fit for modern BS6 cars. Sequential MPI is the floor, not the upgrade.
- **Skipping the RC and insurance endorsement.** It's illegal, it can get the cylinder seized at a traffic stop, and it voids exactly the insurance cover you'd want in a claim.
- **Retrofitting a brand-new in-warranty car.** You trade the factory warranty for the kit's one-to-two-year cover. Wait out the warranty, or buy factory-fitted from day one.
- **Using another city's numbers.** Delhi's ₹86.98/kg tells you nothing about Bengaluru at ₹98 or Hyderabad at ₹112. Always calculate with your own city's rate.
- **Running exclusively on gas.** Driving 100% on CNG to "save more" dries out the petrol side of the system. The 90/10 split costs you almost nothing and keeps everything healthy.
- **Forgetting the recurring dates.** Hydrotest every three years, filter every 10,000–15,000 km. Put them in your phone the day the kit is fitted.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is CNG really cheaper than petrol in 2026?

Yes, at current prices in every major Indian city. In Delhi, ₹86.98/kg at 22–24 km/kg comes to about ₹3.62–₹3.95 per kilometre against petrol's ₹5.67. Even expensive markets like Bengaluru (₹98/kg) stay cheaper per km than petrol. The advantage narrows if your car's CNG mileage is poor or your city's rate is unusually high — which is exactly why step 1 above tells you to calculate it yourself.

### Does CNG damage the engine?

Not when installed properly and maintained normally. The "CNG dries the engine" idea comes from old venturi-era conversions running lean. Modern sequential kits meter fuel precisely, and the standing advice — drive on petrol for roughly 10% of your kilometres and keep your service schedule — addresses the underlying concern. What does hurt an engine is a badly tuned kit or skipping oil changes.

### Is a CNG car safe in an accident?

A legal installation — approved kit, authorised installer, PESO-certified cylinder, hydrotested every three years, endorsed on the RC — is a safe system with pressure relief devices and automatic shut-off. CNG is also lighter than air, so gas from a leak rises and disperses instead of pooling. The genuinely unsafe cars are the undocumented ones, which are illegal regardless of how they drive.

### How long does a CNG cylinder last?

Modern cylinders carry a manufacturer-specified life commonly quoted at 15–20 years, conditional on passing the mandatory hydrotest every three years (about ₹3,000 each). A cylinder that fails testing gets replaced — budget for that possibility on very old cars rather than treating it as a surprise.

### Will a CNG car lose resale value?

Factory-fitted CNG variants generally resell well — buyers recognise the running-cost advantage, and the RC already shows the fuel type. Retrofits resell softer, especially if the paperwork is incomplete. An endorsed, well-documented retrofit from a reputable installer holds value far better than a car whose buyer has to trust the previous owner's story.

### Should I retrofit my existing car or sell and buy a CNG one?

If your petrol car is relatively new or you drive low kilometres, neither — keep it. If it's out of warranty and you drive 15,000+ km a year, retrofitting is usually the cheapest route to the savings (payback around 27,500 km). If you're buying new anyway, a factory-fitted CNG variant gives you the same savings with the warranty, calibration and boot packaging sorted from the factory.

## Final thoughts

CNG isn't better than petrol because it's trendy or because someone told you it's "cheap gas." It's better because of arithmetic: a structurally cheaper raw material, a lighter tax burden per unit of energy, and a per-kilometre cost roughly two rupees below petrol — set against a one-time cost that most city drivers recover inside two years.

The trade-offs are real but small and knowable: a little power, some boot space, a few thousand rupees a year in testing and insurance, and the discipline of doing the paperwork. Whether that exchange is worth it comes down to two honest numbers — how many kilometres you drive, and how long you keep the car.

Run those two numbers through the steps above, and you'll know within ten minutes whether CNG is better than petrol *for you* — and unlike most advice at the dealership, it'll be your own math.
