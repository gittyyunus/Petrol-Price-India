Last year I sat down with six months of fuel receipts because my wife kept asking why we were spending almost ₹6,500 a month on petrol for a car we barely used on weekends. I genuinely thought the number was wrong. Same car, same routes, same office commute — so why was I filling up almost every four days?

Turns out the car wasn't the problem. I was.

I started tracking every fill-up in a simple notes app, noted the odometer reading each time, and calculated my actual mileage instead of trusting the number on the dashboard. My real-world mileage was sitting at 11.8 km/l on a car rated for 18 km/l. That gap was costing me real money every single month.

Over the next three months I changed a handful of habits, tracked the same numbers again, and my mileage climbed to 14.6 km/l. That's roughly a 15% drop in my monthly fuel spend, on the exact same car, same commute, same traffic. No modification, no new tyres, nothing fancy. Just habits.

Here's everything that actually moved the needle, and a few things I tried that turned out to be a waste of time.

## Why your mileage is probably worse than you think

Before we get into the habits, it helps to know why the gap even exists. The mileage figure on your car's brochure (the ARAI-certified number) is tested in controlled lab conditions — steady speed, no traffic signals, no AC, ideal tyre pressure, empty car. Nobody drives like that in real life.

City driving in India involves constant stop-start traffic, potholes, sudden braking, and idling at signals. All of that eats into mileage way more than people realize. So the first step isn't even a habit change — it's just accepting that your real mileage will always be lower than the brochure number, and figuring out how much lower is normal versus how much is you leaving money on the table.

## How to actually measure your own mileage first

You can't improve what you're not measuring. Here's the exact method I used, and I'd genuinely recommend doing this before trying any of the tips below, because otherwise you won't know if anything's actually working.

**Step 1** — Fill your tank completely (to the auto cut-off, not a round number like ₹1000).

**Step 2** — Note your odometer reading at that exact fill-up.

**Step 3** — Drive normally until the tank is empty again, then fill it completely again.

**Step 4** — Note the new odometer reading and how many litres it took to fill up.

**Step 5** — Mileage = (New odometer reading − Old odometer reading) ÷ Litres filled.

Do this for two or three fill-ups to get a stable average, since one tank might include an unusual trip. I used a simple Google Sheet, but apps like Fuelio or DriveSync do this automatically if you log every fill-up through them.

## The habits that actually made a difference

### 1. Smoother acceleration, not slower acceleration

This was my biggest mistake for years. I thought driving fuel-efficiently meant crawling everywhere, which made me an annoying driver in traffic and didn't even help much. The real trick is smooth, gradual acceleration instead of flooring it from every signal.

Aggressive acceleration burns noticeably more fuel because the engine works harder to build speed quickly. I started easing onto the accelerator instead of stomping on it, and reached cruising speed a couple of seconds slower — barely noticeable in normal traffic, but the fuel difference was real.

### 2. Reading traffic ahead instead of reacting to it

This one sounds obvious but almost nobody actually does it. I used to accelerate right up to a red light, then brake hard, then sit idling, then accelerate hard again once it turned green. That's the worst possible pattern for fuel consumption.

Now I watch the signal timers (most Indian traffic signals show a countdown) and the flow of traffic two or three vehicles ahead. If I can see a red light is about to turn, I ease off the accelerator early and let the car coast toward it instead of braking hard at the last second. Less braking means less re-accelerating, which is where most of the fuel actually gets wasted.

### 3. Tyre pressure — checked monthly, not "whenever I remember"

I used to check tyre pressure maybe twice a year, usually when a tyre looked visibly low. Under-inflated tyres increase rolling resistance, which means the engine has to work harder to move the car, which burns more fuel.

I now check tyre pressure once a month using a simple digital pressure gauge (the one I use cost about ₹250), and I follow the pressure specification listed on the sticker inside the driver's door, not the number printed on the tyre itself — those are two different things and I mixed them up initially.

### 4. Cutting unnecessary idling

Living in a city with long traffic signals and frequent school-zone waits, idling adds up more than people expect. If I know I'll be stationary for more than 30-40 seconds (a long red light, waiting to pick someone up), I switch off the engine instead of letting it idle with the AC running.

This one took some getting used to because restarting the engine repeatedly felt odd at first, but modern engines are built to handle this without extra wear, and idling literally burns fuel for zero distance covered.

### 5. Servicing on schedule, especially air filters

I'll admit I used to push servicing a bit later than recommended, usually by a few weeks or even a month, just because it never felt urgent. A clogged air filter forces the engine to work harder to pull in air, which directly hurts mileage. Once I started sticking to the exact service interval and specifically asking the service centre to check the air filter condition every time, I noticed a small but real improvement.

### 6. Not overloading the car

This felt too simple to matter, but it does. Extra weight means the engine works harder for every kilometre. I used to leave random stuff in the boot for weeks — a toolkit, extra water bottles, sports gear — without thinking about it. Clearing out unnecessary weight, especially heavy stuff you're not actually using that day, makes a small but measurable difference, especially on a car with a smaller engine.

### 7. Using AC sensibly, not avoiding it completely

A lot of advice online tells people to just avoid AC entirely to save fuel. I tried this for two weeks in Delhi summer and honestly, it wasn't worth it — at higher speeds, driving with windows down actually increases drag and can hurt mileage more than running the AC would. My approach now is windows up with AC on a moderate setting on highways, and only using windows down at low city speeds where drag isn't really a factor.

## A realistic example of what this adds up to

Let's say you're currently averaging 12 km/l and doing about 1,200 km a month, with petrol around ₹102 per litre.

Current monthly fuel cost: 1,200 ÷ 12 = 100 litres × ₹102 = **₹10,200**

If these habits push your real mileage up to around 13.8 km/l (a roughly 15% improvement, which is exactly what I saw), here's the new number:

1,200 ÷ 13.8 = 87 litres × ₹102 = **₹8,874**

That's a saving of about **₹1,326 a month**, or close to ₹16,000 a year, without spending a single rupee on the car itself.

## Mistakes I made along the way

- **I initially confused "coasting" with "neutral gear coasting."** Some people suggest shifting to neutral while coasting downhill to save fuel — don't do this. It's actually dangerous because you lose engine braking control, and on fuel-injected engines it doesn't even save fuel the way it did on old carburetor cars.
- **I over-focused on AC and under-focused on acceleration.** AC habits matter, but harsh acceleration and braking patterns had a much bigger impact in my own tracking.
- **I trusted the dashboard's fuel efficiency display for a while.** Most onboard trip computers are optimistic by 1-2 km/l compared to the actual calculated mileage from odometer and fuel filled. Always cross-check manually.
- **I ignored wheel alignment for too long.** After hitting a pothole badly, I noticed the car pulling slightly to one side but didn't get it checked for weeks. Poor alignment increases rolling resistance the same way under-inflated tyres do, and I only realized this after a mechanic pointed it out during a routine service.

## Final thoughts

None of these changes felt dramatic on any single day. It wasn't until I compared my tracked numbers after three months that the actual savings became obvious. The habits that mattered most for me were the acceleration pattern and reading traffic ahead — those two alone probably accounted for more than half the improvement.

If you're skeptical (I was too), just track your mileage properly for a couple of weeks using the method above, try two or three of these habits, then track it again. The numbers will tell you honestly whether it's working, and for most city commuters, it genuinely does.
