Last winter I was standing in a CNG queue outside a station near ITO in Delhi. The auto-rickshaw ahead of me had a green leaf sticker on the windscreen, and its engine ticked quietly while it waited. Ten years earlier, that same corner would have had a diesel bus idling beside it, trailing a visible plume of black smoke that you could taste at the back of your throat.

The driver tapped the sticker. "CNG means clean," he said. "Delhi's air used to be terrible. Now it is at least breathable in summer."

He was not wrong — but he was also not telling the whole story. CNG conversion of Delhi's public transport was one of the largest clean-fuel fleet programmes in the world, and it genuinely removed a serious slice of the city's pollution. It did not, however, make Delhi's air clean. Understanding exactly what CNG fixed, what it did not fix, and why the distinction matters is the point of this article.

**The short answer:** Switching automobiles — especially city buses, auto-rickshaws and taxis — from diesel and petrol to CNG cut tailpipe soot, sulphur dioxide, benzene and noise sharply. In Delhi, where the Supreme Court ordered the conversion of the entire public fleet to CNG in the early 2000s, the visible black smoke from diesel buses and autos largely disappeared, and SO2 and toxic hydrocarbon levels fell. But CNG is not zero-emission: it still produces CO2, can emit unburned methane, and does nothing about road dust, construction dust, biomass burning or industrial emissions, which are major reasons Indian cities still struggle with PM2.5.

## Why Indian cities had a vehicle pollution problem

In the mid-1990s, Delhi was routinely described as one of the most polluted cities in the world. A large part of the visible, smelly pollution came from the transport fleet: thousands of diesel buses, auto-rickshaws and taxis burning high-sulphur diesel and older petrol engines with poor combustion. The Centre for Science and Environment's 1996 report *Slow Murder* documented the health damage from vehicular pollution and put the issue on the national agenda.

In 1998, the Supreme Court of India, hearing the long-running M.C. Mehta vehicular pollution case, ordered the conversion of Delhi's public transport fleet to CNG. Buses, autos and taxis were given deadlines that stretched into 2001–02. It was, at the time, one of the largest clean-fuel fleet conversions anywhere in the world.

The logic was straightforward: diesel engines produce visible soot (particulate matter), sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, while petrol engines produce benzene and other toxic aromatics. CNG — mostly methane — burns with almost no soot, contains no benzene or aromatics in the base fuel, and is desulphurised before it enters the gas network. Replacing diesel buses and autos with CNG vehicles therefore attacked the most visible and toxic parts of Delhi's exhaust pollution directly.

## The chemistry: why CNG burns cleaner in a city

The reason CNG helps city air comes down to simple chemistry. Natural gas is mostly methane (CH₄): one carbon atom, four hydrogen atoms. That four-to-one hydrogen-to-carbon ratio is the highest of any common transport fuel, and it is the root of almost every clean-burning property CNG has.

When methane burns completely:

**CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O**

One carbon atom becomes one CO₂ molecule. Petrol is a mixture of longer hydrocarbons (roughly C4 to C12), and diesel is longer still. Burning octane, the textbook petrol molecule:

**2 C₈H₁₈ + 25 O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 18 H₂O**

Three consequences follow directly from methane's simple structure:

| Fuel | Main formula | H:C ratio | CO₂ per unit of energy | Sulphur in base fuel | Aromatics | Soot tendency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CNG | CH₄ (90–95%) | 4:1 | ~55 g/MJ | Essentially none | None | Very low |
| Petrol | C4–C12 mix | ~1.8–2.2:1 | ~72 g/MJ | Up to 10 ppm | 25–35% by volume | Moderate |
| Diesel | C10–C22 mix | ~1.8:1 | ~75 g/MJ | Up to 10 ppm | 10–30% | High |
| LPG / Autogas | C₃H₈ / C₄H₁₀ | ~2.5:1 | ~65 g/MJ | Trace | None | Low |
| Coal (for scale) | C | ~0:1 | ~100 g/MJ | High | Yes | Very high |

**1. No carbon–carbon bonds means almost no soot.** Soot forms when fuel molecules crack apart in a hot, oxygen-poor zone and the carbon fragments clump together. Methane has only one carbon atom, so it has far fewer ways to build those fragments. A well-tuned CNG flame is blue; a rich diesel flame is yellow and sooty. Those soot particles are what made Delhi's diesel buses and autos visibly filthy and dangerous to breathe.

**2. No aromatics means no benzene at the source.** Petrol contains benzene, toluene and xylene to improve octane. Benzene is a known human carcinogen. CNG contains none of them. The difference shows up at the pump as well as the tailpipe: petrol vapour exposes customers and attendants; CNG vapour does not carry the same toxic aromatic load.

**3. No sulphur in the base fuel means almost no SO₂.** Natural gas is desulphurised before it enters the transmission network. The rotten-egg smell at a CNG station is an added odorant (ethyl mercaptan or tetrahydrothiophene) at a few parts per million so leaks can be detected. It contains sulphur, but the quantity is far too small to matter for air quality.

### One correction: CNG is not pure methane

Indian CNG is typically 90–95% methane, with smaller amounts of ethane, propane, butane, nitrogen and CO₂. The exact mix varies by source and by city gas distributor. The differences are small compared with the gap between gas and liquid fuels, but they are real — which is why two CNG cars in two cities can emit slightly different amounts for the same distance.

## Pollutant by pollutant: what actually changed at the tailpipe

The chemistry explains the direction. The emission numbers explain the size. Here is the honest version, with the caveats that brochures leave out:

| Pollutant | Petrol | Diesel | CNG | What it means for city air |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Particulate (PM2.5/PM10) | Low to moderate | High | Very low from combustion | CNG largely removes the visible soot that made diesel exhaust dangerous |
| Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) | Low | Low | Near zero | Removes an acid-rain and respiratory-irritant source |
| Benzene, PAHs | Present | Present | Negligible | No aromatics in the fuel; lower toxic exposure at the pump and tailpipe |
| Carbon monoxide (CO) | Moderate | Low | Low | Methane's gaseous mixing burns more completely, especially at idle |
| NOx | Low | High | Low in modern stoichiometric engines with catalyst | Lean-burn or older CNG engines can be worse; technology and calibration matter |
| CO₂ | Baseline | Often slightly lower per km | Typically 10–25% lower than petrol | Depends on real-world km/kg and engine efficiency |
| Methane (CH₄) | Low | Low | Higher | The main greenhouse-gas trade-off; not a local toxic pollutant |
| Noise | Moderate | High | Lower | Especially noticeable in buses and autos |

A few of these deserve a closer look, because they determine whether CNG actually helped a given city.

**Particulate matter is where CNG wins most clearly.** Diesel's PM problem is structural: long carbon chains, high compression and diffusion combustion create soot. CNG's single-carbon molecule and premixed charge largely avoid it. The remaining PM comes from lubricating oil, trace fuel components and non-exhaust sources such as tyres and brakes. A CNG car is not a zero-PM vehicle, but its combustion PM is a fraction of a diesel's — and in a city where thousands of buses and autos were diesel, that difference was visible within months.

**SO₂ fell because CNG's base gas has essentially none.** Delhi's diesel buses burned fuel with measurable sulphur. Switching to CNG removed that source almost entirely, which is one reason the air smelled different after the conversion.

**NOx is the nuance.** CNG burns hot, and high temperature produces nitrogen oxides. In a modern BS6 stoichiometric engine with a three-way catalyst, NOx is controlled well. In an older lean-burn CNG engine, excess oxygen can make NOx worse than a comparable petrol engine. So "CNG reduces NOx" is true for modern city buses and factory-fitted cars, but not a universal law. The hardware and calibration decide the result.

**CO₂ is the headline, and also the most misunderstood.** CNG produces roughly 25% less CO₂ per unit of energy than petrol or diesel. But per kilometre in a real Indian city car, the saving is usually smaller — roughly 10–25% depending on mileage, engine tuning and whether the car is bi-fuel or dedicated CNG. It is a genuine climate help, but not a miracle.

**Methane is the pollutant nobody puts on the sticker.** A small fraction of CNG passes through the engine unburned (methane slip), and methane can leak from wells, pipelines, compressors and vehicle fuel systems. Methane is roughly 28–30 times as potent as CO₂ over 100 years, and more than 80 times over 20 years. This is the central environmental trade-off of gas, and it is why CNG's climate case depends on keeping the supply chain tight.

## Delhi before and after: what actually changed

The Supreme Court's 1998 order did not clean Delhi's air by itself. What it did was force a fuel and technology switch across the city's most visible polluters: buses, autos and taxis.

CPCB assessments and independent studies by TERI and others documented what happened after the conversion:

- **Visible black smoke became rare.** Diesel buses and autos were the most visible source of soot. Once they ran on CNG, the yellow-black exhaust plume that used to trail ITO, Kashmere Gate and Dhaula Kuan largely disappeared.
- **Sulphur dioxide fell.** Removing high-sulphur diesel from the public fleet took out a major source of respiratory irritation.
- **Toxic hydrocarbons fell.** Benzene and other aromatics vanished from the fuel burned by the city's public fleet.
- **Noise fell.** CNG buses and autos are noticeably quieter than diesel equivalents, which matters on crowded city roads.

That was a genuine public-health achievement. A generation of Indians associates CNG with clean air because Delhi's diesel fleet conversion cut soot and SO₂ sharply.

But Delhi's air did not become clean, and CNG was never designed to make it so. The city still struggles with PM2.5 from construction dust, road dust, biomass burning, industrial emissions and crop-residue burning, and with NOx and ozone from a vehicle fleet that has grown enormously since 2001. CNG reduced **one slice** of the problem — diesel soot from public transport — and did it well. It did not address the other slices.

The honest lesson is this: **CNG earned its reputation by displacing diesel in city fleets. It did not earn a claim of zero pollution, and it was never designed to.**

## Beyond Delhi: CNG in Mumbai, Kolkata, Ahmedabad, Pune and other cities

Delhi is the best-known example, but it is not the only one. CNG has spread across Indian cities, and where it has replaced diesel and petrol in high-mileage commercial vehicles, the same local-air pattern repeats:

- **Mumbai:** Black-and-yellow taxis, BEST buses and thousands of private cars run on CNG, cutting the soot and SO₂ that diesel taxis used to pour onto Marine Drive and the Western Express Highway.
- **Kolkata:** The city's large auto-rickshaw fleet and many taxis converted to CNG, reducing the smoky two-stroke and diesel exhaust that once defined central Kolkata.
- **Ahmedabad, Pune, Hyderabad, Surat and others:** City buses, school buses and commercial fleets increasingly run on CNG, with the same tailpipe benefits — less soot, less SO₂, less noise.

The scale matters. According to the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB), India had **8,916 CNG stations as of 31 March 2026**, up from 6,861 two years earlier and just **738 in 2014**. City gas networks now cover more than 600 cities, and the regulator's target is **18,336 stations by 2030**, with highway corridors explicitly part of the plan.

The vehicle market followed. In FY2026, India sold about **10.34 lakh CNG passenger vehicles**, and CNG's share reached **21.98%** of passenger-vehicle sales — up from 19.60% a year earlier. When one in five new cars runs on gas, and when most city buses, autos and taxis in major metros are gas-powered, the pollution question stops being a niche debate and becomes a mainstream city-air issue.

That is why CNG's urban impact is real: it is not a handful of eco-conscious private car owners. It is fleet-scale displacement of diesel and petrol in the vehicles that drive the most kilometres in the most crowded parts of our cities.

## What CNG did not fix (and cannot fix)

This is the part that rarely makes it into policy brochures, but it is essential for an honest answer.

**CNG addresses exhaust pollution from the vehicles that use it. It does not address:**

- **Road dust and construction dust** — a major share of PM2.5 and PM10 in Indian cities, entirely unrelated to what fuel a car burns.
- **Biomass and crop-residue burning** — seasonal, regional sources that spike northern India's air every winter.
- **Industrial emissions and power plants** — stationary sources outside the tailpipe.
- **Cooking and heating fuels** in some cities.
- **Tyre, brake and road-wear particles** — non-exhaust particulate that CNG vehicles produce just like petrol and diesel vehicles.
- **Growing vehicle numbers** — even if every new car is CNG, a rapidly growing fleet can offset the per-vehicle gains.

CNG is therefore a **targeted intervention, not a complete air-quality strategy.** It cleaned up the vehicle exhaust slice in Delhi and other metros. City-wide PM2.5 still requires dust control, waste management, industrial regulation, cleaner construction practices and stubble-burning solutions. Expecting CNG alone to make a city's air clean is like expecting a good diet to cure every illness — it helps a lot, but it is not everything.

## The honest caveats: when CNG is not clean

The emissions above assume a well-maintained vehicle with a functioning three-way catalyst, a healthy lambda sensor, correct spark plugs and a properly calibrated kit. A badly maintained CNG car can undo much of the benefit:

- **Old venturi kits** meter fuel imprecisely and are a poor match for modern BS6 engines. Sequential injection kits are far cleaner.
- **Removing the catalytic converter** to "improve power" turns a clean car into a dirty one — and it is illegal.
- **Worn spark plugs, a clogged air filter or a drifting lambda sensor** make the engine run rich or lean, hurting both emissions and economy.
- **Methane slip** from the engine and leakage from fuel lines, regulators and the wider gas chain add a greenhouse-gas cost that the tailpipe number does not show.

A CNG car that is serviced properly is a genuinely low-emission vehicle for its class. A CNG car that is neglected is just a petrol car with a different fuel and a green sticker.

## CNG versus the alternatives for city air

The question "what is best for city air?" has no single answer, because the metrics conflict. Here is a qualitative comparison focused on urban pollution:

| Option | Local air quality | Climate | Best urban role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petrol | Moderate (benzene, CO) | Moderate | Low-mileage private use |
| Diesel | Poor in cities (NOx, PM) | Moderate to good | Highway, high-mileage |
| CNG | Good — very low soot, SO₂, benzene | Good if leakage is low | City buses, autos, taxis, fleets |
| LPG / Autogas | Good | Moderate | Niche fleets |
| Petrol hybrid | Good | Good | Efficient city petrol driving |
| EV | Excellent — zero tailpipe | Depends on grid | City use, improving grid |
| Bio-CNG | Excellent | Best in the gas family | Fleets with access to CBG |

For urban PM2.5 and toxic exposure, CNG and EVs are the clear leaders. CNG's advantage over petrol and diesel is that it is available **now**, at scale, with refuelling infrastructure in most major cities, and at a price that makes fleet conversion economic. EVs are better for tailpipe pollution, but they remain expensive, charging infrastructure is still building out, and India's grid is still coal-heavy, so the climate benefit is gradual.

"Cleanest" is not a property of a fuel. It is a property of a fuel, a vehicle, a supply chain and a city's other pollution sources together.

## Keeping a CNG car clean: the practical checklist

If you own or run a CNG vehicle and want it to deserve the "clean" label, the checklist is not exotic:

- Use a **sequential injection kit**, not an old venturi system.
- **Keep the catalytic converter.** Removing it is illegal and turns the car dirty.
- Change **spark plugs** on schedule; CNG burns hotter and wears plugs faster.
- Check the **lambda sensor and fuel trims**; a drifting sensor ruins both emissions and economy.
- Replace the **air filter**; gaseous fuel still needs clean air.
- Use a **low-ash engine oil** and stick to service intervals.
- Get the **PUC (pollution under control) certificate** on time and keep the cylinder **hydrotest** current (every three years).
- Fix any **gas smell immediately.** The odorant is there for a reason; a leak is a safety and climate problem.
- Buy gas from **reputable stations** to avoid moisture and oil carryover that damages regulators and injectors.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did CNG actually reduce pollution in Indian cities?

Yes, but in a specific way. CNG conversion of city buses, autos and taxis sharply cut tailpipe soot, sulphur dioxide, benzene and noise. Delhi's public fleet conversion after the 1998 Supreme Court order is the best-documented case: visible black smoke from diesel buses and autos largely disappeared, and SO₂ and toxic hydrocarbons fell. The same pattern repeats wherever CNG displaces diesel in high-mileage commercial vehicles.

### Which pollutant dropped the most?

Particulate matter (soot) from diesel, followed by sulphur dioxide and toxic aromatics such as benzene. These are the pollutants that made city exhaust visibly dirty and directly harmful to breathe. CNG's advantage on NOx and CO₂ is real but smaller and more dependent on engine technology.

### Is Delhi's air clean now because of CNG?

No. CNG reduced the vehicle-exhaust slice of the problem, but Delhi's PM2.5 still comes heavily from construction dust, road dust, biomass burning, industry and crop residue, and from a much larger vehicle fleet than existed in 2001. CNG is a targeted win, not a complete air-quality solution.

### Does CNG reduce CO₂ compared with petrol and diesel?

Typically yes — about 10–25% less CO₂ per kilometre than petrol in real Indian city driving, and roughly comparable to or slightly better than an efficient diesel. However, upstream methane leakage can erase part of that climate advantage, so the full lifecycle benefit depends on keeping the gas chain tight.

### Is CNG better than an EV for city pollution?

For tailpipe pollution, an EV is better: zero exhaust emissions at the point of use. CNG is more practical today for many fleet operators because of lower upfront cost, fast refuelling and existing infrastructure in most cities. As India's grid gets cleaner, the EV advantage grows; for now, CNG remains the most proven large-scale alternative for city buses, autos and taxis.

### Is every CNG car clean?

No. A well-maintained CNG car with a proper kit, functioning catalyst and regular PUC is genuinely low-emission. A neglected car with a cheap venturi kit, removed catalyst or leaking fuel lines can be dirty. The fuel is only half the system; the other half is the hardware and the mechanic.

## Final thoughts

CNG did not make Indian cities' air clean, and no honest account should claim it did. What it did — and what the evidence clearly supports — is remove a serious, visible and toxic slice of urban pollution: the soot, sulphur dioxide, benzene and noise from diesel buses, autos and taxis that used to define our city centres.

Delhi's conversion after the 1998 Supreme Court order proved the point at scale. The visible black smoke disappeared. The air smelled different. Thousands of commuters and drivers breathed less toxic exhaust. That is a real public-health achievement, and it is why CNG's green sticker means something — but only if we read it accurately.

The mature position is not "CNG is the future" or "CNG is greenwash." It is:

- **Use CNG where it displaces diesel in cities.** The public-health case is strong and proven.
- **Fix the leaks.** Measure methane leakage, regulate it, and stop treating gas loss as an engineering detail.
- **Blend bio-CNG.** That is where the fuel becomes genuinely renewable.
- **Keep vehicles maintained.** A neglected CNG car does not deserve the sticker.
- **Do not call it zero-emission.** It is not, and pretending otherwise undermines the real case for gas.

CNG cleaned up one slice of our cities' pollution. It is up to everything else — dust control, waste management, cleaner industry, better public transport and a greener grid — to clean up the rest.
