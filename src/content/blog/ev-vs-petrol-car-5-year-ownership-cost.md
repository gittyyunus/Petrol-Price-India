A colleague of mine spent about three weekends test driving EVs earlier this year, came back each time more confused than before, and finally just messaged me one night: "just tell me straight, will I actually save money or is this a trap for people who feel guilty about petrol prices."

That question stuck with me because I'd gone through the exact same spiral about a year before him — showroom brochures throwing around "save lakhs," YouTube comments split right down the middle between "best decision ever" and "battery replacement will ruin you," and zero actual math anywhere that accounted for how I really drive.

So I sat down and built the real numbers myself, using a compact SUV comparison since that's the segment most people cross-shop — a Tata Nexon EV against its petrol sibling, running the kind of daily distance a normal city commuter actually does, not some ideal lab figure. Here's everything that came out of that, plus what I'd tell my colleague if he asked me again today.

## The upfront price gap is real, and nobody should pretend otherwise

Let's get this out of the way first because I've seen too many EV-friendly articles gloss over it. A petrol Nexon in a well-equipped variant runs somewhere in the ₹9-11 lakh range on-road. The equivalent Nexon EV, depending on battery size and variant, typically lands ₹1.5 to ₹3 lakh higher on-road.

That gap isn't small pocket change. If you're financing the car, that difference also means a bigger loan and slightly higher EMI from day one. Anyone telling you EVs are "the same price now" either got a specific discount or is talking about a different segment entirely. Go in with your eyes open on this number.

## Where the EV actually starts clawing that money back

This is the part that genuinely surprised me once I ran the numbers properly, because I expected the savings to be modest. They weren't.

**Fuel/charging cost, monthly:**

I used a realistic 1,200 km month — about 40 km a day, which is a pretty normal urban commute plus weekend errands. The petrol Nexon does roughly 13-14 km/litre in real city driving (not the claimed highway figure). At ₹100-105 a litre, that month costs somewhere around ₹9,000-9,600 in fuel.

The Nexon EV, charged at home on a standard domestic tariff around ₹8 per unit, uses roughly 15-17 kWh per 100 km in real-world conditions. Same 1,200 km comes out to about ₹1,400-1,900 for the month.

That's a monthly gap of roughly ₹7,000-8,000, purely on fuel versus electricity. Over a year, that's close to a lakh. Over five years, before touching anything else, you're looking at ₹4-5 lakh in saved running cost — which is more than enough to cover the upfront price difference on its own.

**Important catch here:** that number assumes you're charging at home overnight, which is the cheapest way to do it. If you're relying heavily on public DC fast chargers because you don't have a home charging point, the rate jumps to somewhere between ₹18-24 per unit. Do that regularly and your monthly charging bill can climb to ₹3,000 or more instead of ₹1,500 — still cheaper than petrol, but nowhere near as dramatic a saving. Where you charge matters almost as much as what you drive.

## Maintenance — the quiet second saving

I didn't expect this to matter as much as it did. A petrol engine has an oil filter, engine oil, a clutch (if manual), spark plugs, a cooling system, and a dozen other wear parts that need periodic replacement. Regular servicing on a compact petrol SUV typically runs ₹20,000-40,000 over five years depending on how disciplined you are with the service schedule.

EVs skip almost all of that. No engine oil, no clutch, and regenerative braking means brake pads last noticeably longer since the motor does a chunk of the slowing down instead of the pads. Most estimates put five-year EV maintenance meaningfully lower — commonly cited savings land around ₹50,000 or more compared to the petrol equivalent, mostly from what you simply don't need to replace.

## The one number nobody likes talking about: resale and battery health

Here's where I'd push back on the overly rosy comparisons. EV resale value in India is still a bit of an unknown compared to petrol cars, simply because the used EV market is younger and buyers are cautious about battery health in a used vehicle.

Batteries in modern EVs sold here are mostly LFP chemistry, which tends to degrade more gracefully than older battery types, and manufacturers typically rate them for 7-10 years or roughly 1.5 lakh km before performance drops meaningfully. Full battery replacement, if it ever comes to that, isn't cheap — figure ₹3-6 lakh depending on the pack size, though this is very much a worst-case, end-of-life scenario rather than something you should expect within a normal 5-year ownership window.

My honest take: for a 5-year hold, battery replacement risk is low, but it's still a real unknown that petrol buyers simply don't have to think about, and it's fair to weigh that as a small risk premium against the fuel savings.

## A rough 5-year total, side by side

Putting it together for a compact SUV comparison, assuming home charging and a 12,000-15,000 km a year usage pattern:

- **Upfront price difference:** EV costs roughly ₹1.5-2 lakh more
- **Fuel/charging savings over 5 years:** EV saves roughly ₹4-5 lakh
- **Maintenance savings over 5 years:** EV saves roughly ₹40,000-60,000
- **Net result:** EV owner comes out ahead by roughly ₹1.5-3 lakh over five years, before factoring in any resale difference

The savings are real, but they're not instant — they build up over the ownership period through lower running costs, not at the moment of purchase. If you're planning to sell the car in 2 years instead of 5, the math looks a lot less favourable, since you won't have driven long enough to recover that upfront premium.

## Step-by-step: how to actually work this out for your own situation

1. **Calculate your real monthly distance**, not your best guess. Check your odometer readings over the last few months if you already own a car — most people underestimate this.
2. **Check your actual home electricity tariff slab**, not a generic ₹8/unit assumption. Rates vary quite a bit by state and by how much power your household already uses, since India uses slab-based pricing.
3. **Be honest about whether you can charge at home.** If you live in an apartment without dedicated parking or charger access, factor in public charging rates, which change the math significantly.
4. **Use a proper EV charging cost calculator** — there are several free ones online now that let you enter your exact vehicle, battery size, and local tariff rather than relying on generic averages.
5. **Compare the specific two models you're actually cross-shopping**, not generic "EV vs petrol" numbers. A Nexon EV vs Nexon petrol comparison looks very different from a Creta Electric vs Creta petrol one.
6. **Factor in your realistic ownership period.** If you typically change cars every 2-3 years, run the numbers for that window specifically, not a default 5-year assumption.

## Mistakes I made (and see others make constantly)

I initially compared claimed range figures instead of real-world ones, and ended up overestimating how far I could go on a single charge by almost 15-20%. Always plan around real-world range, not the certified number on the spec sheet.

I also completely ignored home charger installation cost the first time around. A basic Level 1 charger that comes with most EVs is slow but usually free or cheap, but if you want faster home charging with a proper wall box, that's an extra ₹15,000-50,000 depending on the setup — a one-time cost that's easy to forget when you're only looking at per-km running numbers.

The other common mistake — assuming an EV works the same way for long highway trips as it does for city driving. If you regularly do 400-500 km stretches without charging stops, a petrol car is still more practical simply because refuelling takes five minutes and range anxiety is a real thing on routes without dense charging infrastructure. This is genuinely closing fast as highway charging networks expand, but it's not zero today. For someone doing 30-60 km a day around the city and charging at home overnight, this basically never comes up.

## Final thoughts

The EV math genuinely works out in the buyer's favour for the kind of driving most urban Indian car owners actually do — daily commuting, home charging access, and a hold period of 4-5 years or more. That's not marketing spin, that's what the numbers show when you run them honestly.

But it's not a universal yes for everyone. If you're a heavy highway driver without home charging access, or you flip cars every couple of years, the calculation gets a lot tighter and sometimes tips the other way. Run your own numbers with your actual driving pattern before deciding — don't borrow someone's assumptions and expect them to fit your life.

I ended up telling my colleague exactly that. He's driving an EV now, mostly because his commute is short and he has a parking spot with a charger. That's the whole decision, really — not which car is "better," but whether the way you drive actually lines up with what makes an EV cheaper in the first place.
