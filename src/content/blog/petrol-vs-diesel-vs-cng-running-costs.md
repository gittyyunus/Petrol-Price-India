Three years ago I was standing at a car dealership in Delhi, completely stuck. The sales guy kept pushing the CNG variant of the hatchback I liked, saying "sir, aap paisa bachayenge" (you'll save money), while my dad kept saying diesel is what "serious" buyers get. I ended up buying the petrol version out of sheer indecision, and honestly, I regretted not doing the math properly before signing the papers.

So this time around, when I helped my cousin pick her first car last year, I didn't wing it. I actually sat with three months of real fuel bills — mine, my neighbour's diesel Innova, and a colleague's CNG WagonR — and worked out what each fuel type actually costs per kilometre, not just per litre or per kg. The numbers surprised all three of us.

Here's everything I learned, including where the "CNG is always cheaper" advice falls apart.

## Why comparing price per litre is the wrong way to do this

Everyone looks at the pump board and thinks "petrol is ₹102, diesel is ₹95, CNG is ₹83, so CNG wins." That's comparing apples to oranges to a third fruit nobody asked about.

What actually matters is **cost per kilometre**, and that depends on mileage, not just fuel price. A car can have a cheaper fuel but terrible efficiency, and still cost you more to run every month.

The formula is simple:

**Cost per km = Fuel price ÷ Mileage (km per litre or km per kg)**

That's it. Once you calculate this for all three fuels on the same car (or comparable cars), the real picture shows up.

## The actual numbers I worked with

Using Delhi prices as of early August 2026 — petrol around ₹102 per litre, diesel around ₹95 per litre, and CNG around ₹83 per kg (these move slightly every few weeks, so check today's local rate before you rely on this) — here's how a typical hatchback compares:

**Petrol hatchback** (mileage roughly 18 km/l in real city traffic, not the ARAI claimed figure):
₹102 ÷ 18 = **₹5.67 per km**

**Diesel hatchback** (mileage roughly 22 km/l in mixed driving):
₹95 ÷ 22 = **₹4.32 per km**

**CNG hatchback** (mileage roughly 24 km/kg, which is typical for factory-fitted CNG kits):
₹83 ÷ 24 = **₹3.46 per km**

So on paper, CNG wins by a decent margin. But this is where my first mistake comes in.

## The mistake I made: ignoring the upfront cost gap

When I first showed my cousin this table, she almost booked the CNG variant on the spot. Then we actually checked the on-road price difference.

The CNG variant of the car she wanted cost about ₹95,000 more than the petrol version. That's not small change. So I did a second calculation: how many kilometres do you need to drive before that extra ₹95,000 pays for itself through fuel savings?

Difference in running cost between petrol and CNG: ₹5.67 − ₹3.46 = ₹2.21 per km saved

₹95,000 ÷ ₹2.21 = roughly **43,000 km**

If she drives about 12,000 km a year (a realistic number for most city commuters), that's over **3.5 years** before the CNG version actually starts saving her money compared to just buying petrol and pocketing the ₹95,000 upfront. For someone who upgrades cars every 3-4 years, that math genuinely doesn't work in CNG's favour. For someone doing 25,000+ km a year — delivery drivers, cab aggregator drivers, people with long daily commutes — it pays back in under two years and becomes a clear win.

This is the part nobody tells you at the dealership. CNG isn't automatically the smart choice. It's a smart choice **only if you drive enough** to recover the extra cost.

## Diesel's story is even trickier

Diesel used to be the default choice for anyone doing high mileage, but two things have changed this equation over the past few years.

First, the price gap between petrol and diesel has narrowed a lot compared to a decade ago. It used to be a ₹15-20 difference; now it's often ₹6-10 depending on the state and recent excise duty changes.

Second, diesel cars themselves cost more upfront (usually ₹1-1.5 lakh more than the petrol variant of the same model) and have higher maintenance costs — the service intervals involve pricier parts, and BS6 diesel engines need more frequent DPF (diesel particulate filter) attention, which isn't cheap if ignored.

My neighbour's diesel Innova does genuinely well because he drives it over 30,000 km a year for family trips and airport runs. For someone doing typical city commuting under 15,000 km a year, diesel rarely makes financial sense anymore once you factor in the higher purchase price and servicing.

## Step-by-step: how to work this out for your own situation

**Step 1** — Check today's petrol, diesel, and CNG prices for your city. MyPetrolPrice.com, GoodReturns, or your fuel company's app (Indian Oil's "Fuel@IOC" or HP's "HP Pay" app) show live rates and update every morning.

**Step 2** — Find the real-world mileage of the specific model you're comparing, not the ARAI-certified figure. ARAI numbers are tested in lab conditions and almost nobody gets that in actual traffic. Check owner forums like Team-BHP or the model's dedicated Facebook groups — real owners post honest mileage numbers there.

**Step 3** — Calculate cost per km for each fuel option using the formula above.

**Step 4** — Note the on-road price difference between the fuel variants of the same car.

**Step 5** — Divide that price difference by your per-km savings to get your breakeven distance.

**Step 6** — Compare that breakeven distance to your actual annual driving. If you'll cross the breakeven point within 2-3 years of ownership, the higher upfront cost is usually worth it. If it takes 4+ years, you're probably better off with the cheaper upfront option unless you plan to keep the car long-term.

## A few real-world use cases

- **Daily city commuter, 10-12k km/year, keeps car 4-5 years**: Petrol usually wins on total cost of ownership, and it's also the easiest to resell.
- **Cab driver or delivery rider, 40k+ km/year**: CNG almost always wins, and the payback period shrinks to under 18 months.
- **Highway-heavy driver, frequent long trips, 25k+ km/year**: Diesel can still make sense, especially in SUVs and MPVs where the engine is built for sustained torque.
- **Someone who upgrades cars every 2-3 years regardless of mileage**: None of the fuel savings matter much — buy whichever variant has better resale value and lower depreciation in your segment.

## Common mistakes people make

- **Trusting ARAI mileage figures for real calculations.** Always use real owner-reported numbers instead; they're usually 15-25% lower than the claimed figure.
- **Ignoring maintenance cost differences.** CNG kits need periodic checks (roughly every 10,000 km) for leaks and cylinder testing, which has its own cost that people forget to include.
- **Not checking CNG station availability in your area.** This sounds obvious, but I've seen people buy a CNG car and then realise the nearest station involves a 25-minute detour every few days, which eats into the exact savings they bought the car for.
- **Comparing prices across cities without adjusting for local VAT.** Fuel prices vary by state because of different VAT rates, so don't use Delhi numbers to make a decision if you live in Bangalore or Chennai — always check your own city's rate.
- **Forgetting resale value.** Diesel cars in some cities have taken a resale hit due to emission restrictions on older diesel vehicles (like the NGT rulings affecting diesel cars over 10 years old in Delhi-NCR). Factor that into your long-term math too.

## Final thoughts

My cousin ended up going with the petrol variant, not because it was cheaper per kilometre, but because her actual driving pattern (short daily commute, occasional weekend trips) never got her anywhere close to the CNG breakeven point. The dealership pitch made CNG sound like a no-brainer, but the math told a different story once we plugged in her real numbers.

That's really the whole lesson here — there's no universal winner between petrol, diesel, and CNG. There's only the right answer for how much you actually drive, and you won't know that until you run your own numbers instead of trusting the sales pitch.
