Last winter I was standing in a CNG queue in Delhi, the kind where the line curls out of the station and onto the service road. The auto-rickshaw driver in front of me was in no hurry. He had a flask of chai, a folded newspaper on the handlebar, and the calm of a man who has made peace with a twenty-minute wait.

"CNG is clean fuel, sir," he said, when I asked why he had switched. "No pollution."

I asked him what he meant by clean. He tapped the green leaf sticker on his windscreen and laughed. "Everyone says it. Green sticker, clean fuel."

He wasn't wrong. He was repeating a label the whole country has learned — including me, for years. CNG really did clean up a specific and serious part of Delhi's air. But "clean" is a comparative word, not a chemical property, and once you unpack it, the honest answer is more interesting than the sticker.

**The short answer:** CNG is called a clean fuel because it is mostly methane (CH₄), the simplest hydrocarbon. It has the highest hydrogen-to-carbon ratio of any fossil fuel, contains no benzene or aromatics, and produces almost no sulphur dioxide or particulate matter when it burns. Per unit of energy, methane releases roughly 25% less CO2 than petrol and diesel. In a city, that translates into less soot, fewer toxic hydrocarbons and a real improvement in local air quality — which is exactly why Delhi's buses and autos were converted to CNG two decades ago. But CNG is not zero-emission: it still produces CO2, it can emit unburned methane, and its climate advantage depends heavily on how much gas leaks between the well and the nozzle.

That last sentence is the part most "CNG is clean" articles leave out. It is also the part that decides whether the label is deserved.

## What "clean fuel" actually means

The phrase gets used as if it were one thing. It isn't. A fuel can be clean on one measure and dirty on another, and CNG is a good example. There are at least five different yardsticks people mean when they say "clean":

| Yardstick | What it measures | How CNG scores |
|---|---|---|
| Local air pollution | PM2.5, PM10, NOx, CO, hydrocarbons | Very good — especially against diesel |
| Toxic air pollutants | Benzene, 1,3-butadiene, PAHs, metals | Very good — no aromatics in the fuel |
| Greenhouse gases | CO2 per kilometre, methane slip | Good at the tailpipe, variable over the lifecycle |
| Upstream and lifecycle | Extraction, processing, transport, compression | Depends on leakage — the big caveat |
| Renewable content | Share of fuel from biological or renewable sources | Zero for fossil CNG; high for bio-CNG |

By that scorecard, CNG is excellent on local pollution and toxics, good but not miraculous on CO2, variable on lifecycle, and not renewable at all unless it is bio-CNG. That is the whole argument in one paragraph.

The green sticker is a summary of the first two rows. It says nothing about the last three.

## The chemistry: methane is the simplest hydrocarbon, and that is the point

Natural gas is a mixture, but its main component is methane: one carbon atom and four hydrogen atoms. That ratio — four hydrogens for every carbon — is the highest of any fossil fuel, and it is the root of almost every clean-burning property CNG has.

When methane burns completely, the reaction is almost embarrassingly simple:

**CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O**

One molecule of methane produces one molecule of CO2. Petrol is a mixture of longer hydrocarbons — roughly C4 to C12 — and diesel is longer still. Burning octane, the textbook petrol molecule, looks like this:

**2 C₈H₁₈ + 25 O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 18 H₂O**

Because methane packs four hydrogen atoms around every carbon, it releases more energy for each molecule of CO2 it produces. On a lower-heating-value basis, methane gives roughly 800 kJ per mole of CO2, against about 630–680 kJ for petrol. That is the thermodynamic reason a gas flame can deliver the same power with less carbon — and why the CO2 advantage shows up even before you account for engine efficiency.

Here is how the fuels compare on the properties that matter for combustion:

| Fuel | Main formula | H:C ratio | CO2 per unit of energy | Sulphur in base fuel | Aromatics | Soot tendency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CNG | CH₄ (90–95%) | 4:1 | ~55 g/MJ | Essentially none | None | Very low |
| Petrol | C4–C12 mix | ~1.8–2.2:1 | ~72 g/MJ | Up to 10 ppm | 25–35% by volume | Moderate |
| Diesel | C10–C22 mix | ~1.8:1 | ~75 g/MJ | Up to 10 ppm | 10–30% | High |
| LPG / Autogas | C₃H₈ / C₄H₁₀ | 2.7:1 / 2.5:1 | ~65 g/MJ | Trace | None | Low |
| Coal (for scale) | C | ~0:1 | ~100 g/MJ | High | Yes | Very high |

Three things fall out of that table:

**1. No carbon-carbon bonds means little soot.** Soot forms when fuel molecules break apart in a hot, oxygen-poor zone and the carbon fragments clump together. Methane has only one carbon atom, so it has far fewer ways to build those fragments. A well-tuned CNG flame is blue; a rich petrol or diesel flame is yellow and sooty. That is not a cosmetic difference — those soot particles are what make diesel exhaust dangerous.

**2. No aromatics means no benzene at the source.** Petrol contains benzene, toluene, xylene and other aromatic compounds because they improve octane and energy density. Benzene is a known human carcinogen. CNG contains none of them. The difference shows up not just at the tailpipe but at the pump, where petrol vapour exposes both customers and attendants.

**3. No sulphur in the base fuel means almost no SO2.** Natural gas is desulphurised before it enters the transmission network. The rotten-egg smell at a CNG station is an added odorant — usually ethyl mercaptan or tetrahydrothiophene — present at a few parts per million so leaks can be detected. It contains sulphur, but the quantity is far too small to matter for air quality. If someone tells you CNG is "sulphur-free," the precise version is: the base gas is, and the odorant is a trace.

### One correction: CNG is not pure methane

Indian CNG is typically 90–95% methane, with smaller amounts of ethane, propane, butane, nitrogen and CO2. The exact mix varies by source and by city gas distributor. Imported LNG can have a different composition from domestic APM gas, and that changes the Wobbe index, the methane number and — slightly — the CO2 and particulate output.

This is why two CNG cars in two cities can emit slightly different amounts for the same distance. It is also why "CNG" is not a single chemical specification. The differences are small compared with the gap between gas and liquid fuels, but they are real, and they matter if you are comparing emissions data from different studies.

## Pollutant by pollutant: what actually comes out of the tailpipe

The chemistry explains the direction. The emission numbers explain the size. Here is the honest version, with the caveats that the brochure leaves out:

| Pollutant | Petrol | Diesel | CNG | The honest note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO2 | Baseline | Often slightly lower per km | Typically 10–25% lower than petrol | Depends on real-world km/kg and engine efficiency |
| CO | Moderate | Low | Low, especially at idle | A healthy three-way catalyst makes all three low |
| NMHC | Baseline | Lower | Lower | Methane is excluded from NMHC because it is not reactive — but it is a greenhouse gas |
| NOx | Low | High | Low in stoichiometric engines with a catalyst | Lean-burn CNG can be worse; the technology matters |
| PM2.5 | Low to moderate (worse with direct injection) | High | Very low from combustion | Tyre, brake and road dust remain |
| SO2 | Low | Low | Near zero | The odorant is a trace, not a load |
| Benzene, PAHs | Present | Present | Negligible | No aromatics in the fuel |
| Methane | Low | Low | Higher | The main greenhouse-gas trade-off |
| Noise | Moderate | High | Lower | Especially noticeable in buses and autos |

A few of these deserve a closer look.

**CO2 is the headline, and also the most misunderstood.** CNG's advantage per unit of energy is genuine and large: about 25% less CO2 than petrol and diesel for the same amount of heat. But a car does not run on heat; it runs on useful work. A bi-fuel CNG engine has to keep a compression ratio low enough to run on petrol, and methane displaces some of the air in the cylinder, so the engine often produces less power and uses more energy per kilometre than it would on petrol. The per-kilometre CO2 saving is therefore smaller than the per-megajoule saving. I will do the arithmetic in the next section.

**CO and hydrocarbons fall because the fuel burns more completely.** Methane's simple structure and gaseous mixing mean less unburned fuel escapes, particularly at idle. A modern closed-loop system with a three-way catalyst keeps CO and NMHC extremely low on all three fuels, so the difference is largest on older vehicles and at cold start.

**NOx is the nuance most articles miss.** CNG burns at a high temperature, and high temperature is exactly what produces nitrogen oxides. In a stoichiometric engine with a three-way catalyst, NOx is controlled well. In an older lean-burn CNG engine, excess oxygen can make NOx worse than a comparable petrol engine. This is why "CNG reduces NOx" is true for modern BS6 cars but not a universal law. The hardware and calibration decide the result.

**Particulate matter is where CNG wins most clearly.** Diesel's PM problem is structural: long carbon chains, high compression, and diffusion combustion create soot. CNG's single-carbon molecule and premixed charge largely avoid it. The remaining PM comes from lubricating oil, trace fuel components and non-exhaust sources such as tyres and brakes. A CNG car is not a zero-PM vehicle, but its combustion PM is a fraction of a diesel's.

**Methane is the pollutant nobody puts on the sticker.** Methane is not toxic and is not regulated as a local air pollutant in the same way as NOx or PM. But it is a powerful greenhouse gas, and a small fraction of the fuel passes through the engine unburned. That unburned methane — often called methane slip — is the environmental cost of gas that rarely makes it into the sales brochure.

## The carbon math: why "25% less CO2" is both true and misleading

Let's do the calculation properly, because this is where the label earns or loses its credibility.

The carbon content of the fuel is fixed:

- Petrol: about **2.31 kg CO2 per litre**
- Diesel: about **2.68 kg CO2 per litre**
- CNG: about **2.75 kg CO2 per kilogram**
- LPG: about **3.0 kg CO2 per kilogram**

Per unit of energy, the picture is even clearer:

| Fuel | Energy content | CO2 per MJ |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol | ~32 MJ/litre | ~72 g |
| Diesel | ~36 MJ/litre | ~75 g |
| CNG | ~50 MJ/kg | ~55 g |
| LPG | ~46 MJ/kg | ~65 g |

So methane is about **24% less carbon-intensive than petrol** and **27% less than diesel** for the same energy. That is the source of the famous "25% cleaner" claim.

But your car does not buy energy in megajoules. It buys kilometres. Here is the same calculation using realistic Indian city mileage for a typical hatchback:

| Fuel | Fuel price (Delhi, Oct 2026) | Realistic mileage | CO2 per km |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petrol | ₹102.12/litre | 18 km/litre | **128 g** |
| Diesel | ~₹95/litre | 22 km/litre | **122 g** |
| CNG | ₹86.98/kg | 24 km/kg | **115 g** |
| CNG | ₹86.98/kg | 28 km/kg | **98 g** |
| LPG | ~₹75/kg | 21 km/kg | **143 g** |

At 24 km/kg, CNG emits about **11% less CO2 per kilometre than petrol** and about **6% less than an efficient diesel**. At 28 km/kg, the gap widens to **23% less than petrol** and **19% less than diesel**. The 25% headline is the top end of the range, not the average.

Why the gap between the per-MJ and per-km numbers? Because CNG's engine efficiency is often lower in a bi-fuel car. Methane takes up space in the cylinder that air would otherwise occupy, the compression ratio is held back by petrol operation, and the flame burns more slowly. A dedicated CNG engine can recover some of that loss with a higher compression ratio and optimised combustion, which is why factory CNG cars often do better than aftermarket conversions.

Over a year of driving, the difference is real but not enormous:

| Annual distance | Petrol at 128 g/km | CNG at 24 km/kg | CNG at 28 km/kg | Saving vs petrol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 km | 1.28 t CO2 | 1.15 t | 0.98 t | 0.13–0.30 t |
| 15,000 km | 1.92 t | 1.72 t | 1.47 t | 0.20–0.45 t |
| 20,000 km | 2.56 t | 2.29 t | 1.96 t | 0.27–0.60 t |

Fleet-average estimates often quote a bigger gap — around 190 g/km for petrol and 143 g/km for CNG, a saving of about 0.7 tonnes a year at 15,000 km. That is plausible for a larger car or a dedicated CNG engine, but it is not a universal number. The lesson is not that CNG is dirty. The lesson is that **"clean" has a range, and the range is wide enough to change the policy argument.**

### Why city driving flatters CNG

If most of your driving is stop-go, CNG's advantages compound. At idle and low load, petrol engines run rich and produce CO and unburned hydrocarbons; CNG's gaseous mixing and simpler molecule burn more completely. Diesel engines produce NOx and PM in the same conditions, and their aftertreatment systems need heat to work. CNG has no such penalty.

On an open highway, the picture shifts. Diesel's high compression and lean burn give it a real efficiency advantage, and a modern diesel can be competitive with CNG on CO2 per kilometre. CNG's advantage is largest where Indian cars spend most of their time: in traffic.

## Why Delhi made CNG famous

The "clean fuel" label in India is not a laboratory invention. It came from a public-health emergency and a court order.

In the mid-1990s, Delhi was routinely described as one of the most polluted cities in the world. Diesel buses and auto-rickshaws were a visible part of the problem: black smoke, fine particulate matter, and sulphur dioxide. The Centre for Science and Environment's 1996 report *Slow Murder* documented the health damage from vehicular pollution and put the issue on the national agenda. In 1998, the Supreme Court of India, hearing the long-running M.C. Mehta case, ordered the conversion of Delhi's public transport fleet to CNG. Buses, autos and taxis were given deadlines that stretched into 2001–02.

It was, at the time, one of the largest clean-fuel fleet programmes in the world. And it worked on its own terms:

- **Particulate matter from the vehicle fleet fell sharply.** The visible black smoke from diesel buses and autos became rare.
- **Sulphur dioxide fell.** CNG's near-zero sulphur content removed a major source of acid rain and respiratory irritation.
- **Toxic hydrocarbons fell.** Benzene and other aromatics disappeared from the fuel burned by the city's public fleet.
- **Noise fell.** CNG buses and autos are noticeably quieter than their diesel equivalents.

That is a genuine public-health achievement, and it is why a generation of Indians associates CNG with clean air. The green leaf sticker is a relic of a real victory.

But Delhi's air did not become clean. The city still struggles with PM2.5 from construction dust, road dust, biomass burning, industry and crop-residue burning, and with nitrogen oxides and ozone from a vehicle fleet that has grown enormously since 2001. CNG reduced one slice of the problem — diesel soot — and did it well. It did not address the other slices, and it did not make the fuel zero-impact.

The honest lesson is this: **CNG earned the "clean fuel" label by displacing diesel in city fleets. It did not earn a claim of zero pollution, and it was never designed to.**

### The infrastructure that made it mainstream

For years, the practical objection to CNG was simple: there was nowhere to fill it. That has changed faster than most people realise.

According to the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board, India had **8,916 CNG stations as of 31 March 2026**, up from 6,861 two years earlier and just **738 in 2014**. City gas networks now cover more than 600 cities, and the regulator's target is **18,336 stations by 2030**, with highway corridors explicitly part of the plan.

The vehicle market followed. In FY2026, India sold about **10.34 lakh CNG passenger vehicles**, and CNG's share reached **21.98%** of passenger-vehicle sales — up from 19.60% a year earlier and roughly 12–13% three years ago. When one in five new cars runs on gas, the clean-fuel question stops being a niche debate and becomes a mainstream policy question.

That scale is also why the methane problem matters more now than it did in 2001. A few thousand CNG buses leaking a little gas is a rounding error. Ten lakh new CNG cars a year, supplied by a growing import-dependent gas chain, is a climate variable worth measuring.

## The catch: methane is a greenhouse gas, and it leaks

Here is the part that should be on the green sticker, in smaller print.

Methane is a far more potent greenhouse gas than CO2. Over 100 years, one kilogram of methane traps roughly **28–30 times** as much heat as one kilogram of CO2. Over 20 years — the window that matters for avoiding near-term tipping points — the multiplier is **more than 80**. Methane's atmospheric lifetime is only about a decade, which is why cutting it produces relatively fast climate benefits, and why leaking it is so costly.

Now run the arithmetic on a single tonne of CNG:

- Burning one tonne of methane produces about **2.75 tonnes of CO2**.
- If **2%** of that gas leaks somewhere between the well and the nozzle, you lose 20 kg of methane.
- At a 100-year global warming potential of 28, those 20 kg are equivalent to **560 kg of CO2**.
- Total lifecycle emissions: about **3.31 tonnes of CO2e** instead of 2.75 tonnes — a **20% increase**.
- At **3%** leakage, the increase is about **30%**.

CNG's tailpipe CO2 advantage over petrol is roughly 10–25%. A leakage rate in the low single digits can therefore erase much or all of it. That is not a fringe concern; it is the central caveat in the climate case for natural gas.

Where does the leakage come from?

- **Upstream production:** wellheads, gathering lines, processing plants and compressors.
- **Transmission:** long-distance pipelines, valves, flanges and maintenance vents.
- **City gas distribution:** local networks, pressure-reducing stations and station equipment.
- **Compression and dispensing:** CNG stations use energy to compress gas to 200–250 bar, and the equipment can leak.
- **Imported LNG:** liquefaction, shipping, boil-off and regasification add more stages, each with its own losses.
- **The vehicle itself:** fuel lines, regulators, injectors and the engine's unburned methane.

India does not yet have comprehensive, publicly reported measurement of methane leakage across its gas chain. Studies in the United States and Europe have found leakage rates ranging from a fraction of a percent to several percent, with a small number of "super-emitters" responsible for a disproportionate share. Satellite monitoring has made those super-emitters easier to spot, but India-specific data remains thin.

Then there is **methane slip** from the engine. Methane is chemically stable and burns slowly; a small fraction can survive combustion and exit the tailpipe. Modern stoichiometric engines with close-coupled catalysts reduce it, but they do not eliminate it. Older lean-burn engines, poorly tuned conversions and dual-fuel engines tend to be worse.

This is why the phrase "CNG is clean" needs a qualifier: **CNG is clean at the point of combustion if the engine is well designed, and clean across the lifecycle only if the gas chain is tight.** The fuel is not the whole system.

### What would change my mind

I am not arguing that CNG is a lie. I am arguing that the label is conditional. The conditions are measurable:

1. **Leakage below about 1%** of gas produced and delivered, verified by independent measurement rather than engineering estimates.
2. **Methane slip near zero** in the vehicles actually sold, not just in the type-approval test.
3. **A growing share of bio-CNG** in the blend, so the methane comes from waste rather than fossil deposits.

If India can show those three numbers, the climate case for CNG is strong. If it cannot, the honest claim is narrower: CNG is a proven way to cut urban soot and toxic exposure, with a climate benefit that depends on fixing the gas chain.

## Well-to-wheel: a more honest comparison

Tailpipe numbers are only half the story. A well-to-wheel comparison includes extraction, processing, transport, compression or refining, and combustion. The exact result depends on where the fuel comes from and how the electricity is made, so treat this as a set of ranges, not a league table:

| Option | Indicative lifecycle CO2e per km | What drives the range |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol car | 210–240 g | Crude source, refining, engine efficiency |
| Diesel car | 190–220 g | Refining, high efficiency, aftertreatment |
| CNG car, low leakage | 150–180 g | Domestic gas, tight network, efficient engine |
| CNG car, high leakage | 200–250 g | Imported LNG, old infrastructure, methane slip |
| Bio-CNG car | 40–100 g, potentially lower | Feedstock, digestate handling, upgrading energy |
| Small EV, India grid | 100–160 g | Grid carbon intensity, battery manufacturing, size |

These numbers are indicative, and the boundaries matter. A CNG car running on domestic APM gas in a city with a modern network sits at the low end. A CNG car running on imported LNG in a city with ageing compressors sits at the high end. An EV on India's coal-heavy grid is not zero-carbon either; it moves the emissions to the power plant, though it does improve local air quality.

The fairest conclusion is not "CNG is cleaner than everything" or "CNG is a fossil fuel, so it is not clean." It is: **CNG is a transitional fuel with a real urban-air benefit and a climate benefit that is conditional on leakage and efficiency.** That is a more useful sentence than either slogan.

## Bio-CNG: the version that actually deserves the label

If you want a genuinely clean version of CNG, the answer is bio-CNG — also called compressed biogas, or CBG.

The process is straightforward in principle. Agricultural residue, cattle dung, food waste, sewage sludge or municipal organic waste is fed into an anaerobic digester. Bacteria break it down and produce biogas, which is roughly 50–60% methane and 40–50% CO2. The biogas is cleaned and upgraded to more than 90% methane, compressed, and sold as CBG. The residue becomes digestate, a fertiliser substitute.

The climate case is different from fossil CNG in three ways:

- **The carbon was recently in the atmosphere.** The plants that produced the feedstock absorbed CO2 as they grew, so the CO2 released when the biogas burns is part of a short cycle rather than ancient carbon dug out of the ground.
- **Methane is captured instead of released.** If the same waste had decomposed in a landfill or a open pond, it would have emitted methane directly. Capturing it and burning it converts a potent greenhouse gas into CO2 and useful energy.
- **Waste is diverted from landfills and burning.** That reduces local pollution and creates a rural income stream.

The Government of India's SATAT scheme, launched in 2018, set an ambitious target of 5,000 CBG plants. Progress has been slower than planned — feedstock logistics, land, financing and offtake agreements are all harder than they look — but the policy direction is clear. The government has also notified a phased CBG blending obligation, starting at 1% and scheduled to rise to 5% by 2028–29, which would gradually introduce renewable methane into the same CNG network.

There is also a hydrogen pathway. HCNG — a blend of hydrogen and CNG, typically around 18% hydrogen in Indian trials — can lower CO2 and slightly improve combustion, though it adds cost and complexity. It is a useful bridge technology, not a silver bullet.

The important point for the "clean fuel" debate is this: **the methane molecule is the same whether it comes from a gas field or a dairy farm, but the lifecycle carbon is not.** Bio-CNG is where the label becomes more than a relative claim.

## CNG versus the alternatives: there is no single "cleanest" fuel

The question "is CNG cleaner than petrol/diesel/EV?" has no single answer because the metrics conflict. Here is a qualitative comparison:

| Option | Local air quality | Climate | Toxic exposure | Infrastructure | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Petrol | Moderate | Moderate | High (benzene, aromatics) | Universal | Convenience, low mileage |
| Diesel | Poor in cities (NOx, PM) | Moderate to good | Moderate | Universal | High-mileage highway driving |
| CNG | Good | Good if leakage is low | Low | Growing fast | City cars, buses, autos, fleets |
| LPG / Autogas | Good | Moderate | Low | Limited | Niche fleets |
| Petrol hybrid | Good | Good | Moderate | Universal | Efficient petrol driving |
| EV | Excellent | Depends on grid | Excellent | Growing | City use, clean grid |
| Bio-CNG | Excellent | Best in the gas family | Low | Limited | Fleets with access to CBG |

For urban PM2.5 and toxic exposure, CNG and EVs are the clear leaders. For climate, an EV on a clean grid or a bio-CNG car is better than fossil CNG; a fossil CNG car is better than petrol only if leakage is controlled. For cost and refuelling convenience, petrol and diesel still win. For city fleets that drive all day, CNG is the most practical proven alternative.

"Cleanest" is not a property of a fuel. It is a property of a fuel, a vehicle, a supply chain and a use case together.

## A badly maintained CNG car is not clean

This is the part that rarely makes it into policy documents, but it matters for anyone who owns or buys a CNG car.

The emissions above assume a well-maintained vehicle with a functioning three-way catalyst, a healthy lambda sensor, correct spark plugs and a properly calibrated kit. A poorly maintained CNG car can emit more CO and hydrocarbons than a well-maintained petrol car, and its methane slip can be worse. The fuel is only half the system; the other half is the hardware and the mechanic.

If you want your CNG car to live up to the label, the checklist is not exotic:

- **Use a sequential injection kit, not an old venturi system.** Venturi kits are cheaper and cruder; they meter fuel imprecisely and are a poor match for modern BS6 engines.
- **Keep the catalytic converter.** Removing it to "improve power" turns a clean car into a dirty one. It is also illegal.
- **Change spark plugs on schedule.** CNG burns hotter and drier than petrol and wears plugs faster. Use the correct plug and gap.
- **Check the lambda sensor and fuel trims.** A drifting sensor makes the engine run rich or lean, which hurts both emissions and economy.
- **Replace the air filter.** Gaseous fuel still needs clean air; a clogged filter richens the mixture.
- **Use a low-ash engine oil and stick to the service interval.** CNG engines run hot, and oil degradation is real.
- **Check valve clearance at major services.** Older conversions without hardened valve seats can suffer valve recession.
- **Run about 10% of your kilometres on petrol.** It keeps the petrol-side injectors, pump and seals exercised, and it is the simplest answer to the "CNG dries the engine" concern.
- **Buy gas from reputable stations.** Moisture and oil carryover in poor-quality gas can damage regulators and injectors.
- **Fix any gas smell immediately.** The odorant is there for a reason. A leak is a safety and climate problem, not a normal CNG quirk.
- **Keep the hydrotest and PUC up to date.** The cylinder test is every three years; the pollution certificate is part of the legal compliance chain.

A CNG car that is serviced properly is a genuinely low-emission vehicle for its class. A CNG car that is neglected is just a petrol car with a different fuel and a green sticker.

## Myths vs facts

| Myth | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| "CNG is zero-emission." | It produces CO2 and can emit unburned methane. It has no lead, benzene or aromatics, and very low PM, but it is not zero-emission. |
| "CNG causes no pollution at all." | Combustion PM is very low, but tyre, brake and road dust remain, and CO2 is still a greenhouse gas. |
| "CNG is always better for the climate than petrol." | It usually is at the tailpipe, but upstream methane leakage can erase the advantage. Low leakage is the condition. |
| "CNG is worse than diesel because of methane." | It depends on the leakage rate and the vehicle. CNG is clearly better for urban PM and NOx in modern engines; the climate comparison is closer. |
| "CNG is a renewable fuel." | Fossil CNG is not renewable. Bio-CNG (CBG) is, and it is being blended into the network in small volumes. |
| "CNG has no sulphur." | The base gas is desulphurised. The smell is an added sulphur-based odorant at trace concentrations. |
| "CNG is just a green sticker." | The sticker commemorates a real public-health win: Delhi's diesel fleet conversion cut soot and SO2 sharply. |
| "CNG is unsafe." | A legal, type-approved, properly tested installation is safe. An undocumented roadside kit is not. See the safety section in [why CNG is better than petrol](/blog/why-cng-is-better-than-petrol/). |
| "CNG is always cheaper per kilometre." | Usually, but not always. It depends on your city's price, your car's km/kg, and the upfront kit or variant premium. |

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is CNG called a clean fuel?

Because it is mostly methane, the simplest hydrocarbon, with a high hydrogen-to-carbon ratio and no benzene, aromatics or sulphur in the base fuel. It burns almost completely, producing very little particulate matter, carbon monoxide or toxic hydrocarbons. Per unit of energy it produces about 25% less CO2 than petrol or diesel, and in cities it has a proven record of displacing diesel soot. "Clean" is a comparative label, not a claim of zero emissions.

### Is CNG really pollution-free?

No. CNG still produces CO2, the main greenhouse gas, and a small amount of unburned methane. It also produces trace amounts of NOx and non-exhaust particulate matter from tyres and brakes. What it largely avoids is the soot, sulphur dioxide, benzene and PAH emissions that make diesel and petrol exhaust harmful to urban air.

### Is CNG cleaner than petrol and diesel?

For local air pollution — PM2.5, toxic hydrocarbons and SO2 — yes, clearly, especially compared with diesel. For CO2, it is typically 10–25% lower per kilometre than petrol, and roughly comparable to or slightly better than an efficient diesel. For the climate overall, the answer depends on methane leakage across the gas supply chain, which is why the label needs a lifecycle qualifier.

### Does CNG emit methane?

Yes. A small fraction of the fuel passes through the engine unburned, and methane can also leak from wells, pipelines, compressors and vehicle fuel systems. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas — roughly 28–30 times CO2 over 100 years, and more than 80 times over 20 years. This is the main environmental trade-off of switching from liquid fuels to gas.

### Is CNG bad for global warming?

Not inherently, but it is not automatically good either. Burning gas produces less CO2 per unit of energy than petrol or diesel, so a CNG car with low leakage and good efficiency has a lower lifecycle carbon footprint. If leakage is high, the methane released upstream can outweigh the CO2 saved at the tailpipe. The right question is not "is CNG good?" but "how much methane leaks in the chain that supplies my city?"

### What is bio-CNG, and is it cleaner?

Bio-CNG, or compressed biogas (CBG), is methane produced from agricultural waste, cattle dung, food waste or sewage. It is upgraded to pipeline quality and compressed. It is cleaner because the carbon came from recent plant growth, the methane is captured instead of released, and the waste is diverted from landfills. It can be close to carbon-neutral and, in some cases, carbon-negative on a lifecycle basis.

### Is CNG cleaner than an EV?

It depends on the metric and the grid. An EV has zero tailpipe emissions and is clearly better for local air quality. Its lifecycle CO2 depends on how the electricity is made; on India's coal-heavy grid, a small EV is roughly comparable to a efficient hybrid or a low-leakage CNG car, and it improves as the grid decarbonises. CNG has the advantage of fast refuelling and a lower upfront cost for many buyers; the EV has the advantage of zero tailpipe pollution and lower running costs.

### Does CNG reduce PM2.5 in Indian cities?

It reduces the vehicle share of PM2.5 significantly when it displaces diesel, as Delhi's bus and auto conversion showed. It does not solve city-wide PM2.5, because dust, construction, biomass burning, industry and crop-residue burning are also major sources. CNG is a targeted intervention, not a complete air-quality strategy.

### Is a CNG car safe?

A factory-fitted or legally retrofitted CNG car with a type-approved kit, a PESO-certified cylinder, a hydrotest every three years and an RC endorsement is safe. CNG is lighter than air, so leaks disperse upward rather than pooling, and the system has pressure-relief and shut-off devices. The dangerous cases are undocumented roadside conversions. The full safety checklist is in [why CNG is better than petrol](/blog/why-cng-is-better-than-petrol/).

### How can I tell if my CNG car is running clean?

Get the pollution certificate on time, scan the OBD for fuel-trim and catalyst codes, keep the spark plugs and air filter fresh, and fix any gas smell immediately. A well-maintained CNG car should idle smoothly, start easily on both fuels and return consistent km/kg. If the mileage drops suddenly or the exhaust smells strongly of unburned gas, get the kit and the catalyst checked.

## Final thoughts

CNG is called a clean fuel because it is cleaner — not because it is clean. The distinction matters.

It is cleaner than diesel in the ways that matter most for a crowded city: almost no soot, no sulphur dioxide, no benzene at the pump, and lower noise. It is cleaner than petrol on CO2 per unit of energy, and usually cleaner per kilometre, though the gap is smaller in the real world than the brochure suggests. It is cleaner than both on toxic exposure.

But it is still a fossil fuel. It still produces CO2. It can leak methane at every stage from the wellhead to the fuel line, and that methane is a far more potent greenhouse gas than the CO2 it saves. The label is earned at the tailpipe and must be defended across the entire chain.

The mature position is not "CNG is the future" or "CNG is a greenwash." It is:

- **Use CNG where it displaces diesel in cities.** The public-health case is strong and proven.
- **Fix the leaks.** Measure them, regulate them, and stop treating methane loss as an engineering detail.
- **Blend bio-CNG.** That is where the fuel becomes genuinely renewable.
- **Keep the car maintained.** A neglected CNG car does not deserve the sticker.
- **Do not call it zero-emission.** It isn't, and pretending otherwise undermines the real case for gas.

The next time you see the green leaf, read it as "cleaner than the fuel it replaced" rather than "clean." That is a smaller claim, but it is the one the evidence supports — and it is still a claim worth making.
