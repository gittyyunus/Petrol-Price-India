Last month a friend asked me the question I get asked more than any other: *"Bhai, kitna kharcha aata hai chalane mein?"* — how much does it actually cost to run your car? And without thinking, I answered the way most of us do: "Petrol is about ₹102 a litre right now."

That answer is worthless. ₹102 per litre tells you the price of fuel — it tells you nothing about the cost of *distance*. A litre means nothing until you know how many kilometres it buys you. A ₹102 litre that covers 18 kilometres is a completely different expense from a ₹102 litre that covers 11 kilometres, and neither number helps you decide anything until you convert it into the one figure that actually matters: **cost per kilometre**.

So I sat down, worked out the math for bikes, scooters, hatchbacks and SUVs at today's prices, tested the two methods of measuring it against each other, and wrote down the whole thing here. By the end of this article you'll have your own exact number — and if you want it without doing the division yourself, our [fuel cost calculator](/calculator) gives it to you in about two minutes, using live prices for your city.

## Why the pump board price tells you nothing

Every fuel price article in India — including several on this site — starts with a number like "petrol in Delhi costs ₹102.12 per litre on 12 October 2026." That number is useful context. It is useless as a personal expense metric, for three reasons.

**First, units don't match.** You don't buy litres for fun — you buy *kilometres*. Your bike, your car, your office commute, your weekend trip — everything in your fuel life is measured in distance. A price per litre is the intermediate step, not the destination.

**Second, mileage swamps the price.** The difference between a 45 km/l commuter bike and an 11 km/l petrol SUV is a factor of four — far more than the price difference between any two cities or any two fuels. A bike rider in expensive Mumbai still spends less per kilometre than a car owner in cheap Delhi. Price per litre ranks cities; cost per kilometre ranks *vehicles and budgets*.

**Third, only cost per km lets you predict.** "How much will this job commute cost me every month?" "Can I afford a road trip to Jaipur?" "Is it cheaper to drive or take a cab?" None of these questions can be answered with ₹102. All of them collapse into one multiplication once you have your cost per km.

The rest of this article is about getting that number — accurately — in three different ways, depending on how precise you want to be.

## The formula

Here it is, in full:

**Cost per km = Petrol price per litre ÷ Mileage (km per litre)**

That's the entire core of it. If your petrol costs ₹102.12 a litre and your car returns 18 km/l in real driving, you're paying ₹5.67 for every kilometre you drive. Everything else in this article is about the two hard parts of that equation: getting a *real* mileage figure, and getting a *real* price for your city.

## Method 1: the simple formula (30 seconds, good enough for most decisions)

This is the method to use when you want a quick answer — comparing two cars you're thinking of buying, estimating a road trip budget, or settling a debate about whether your colleague's "amazing mileage" claim holds up.

**Step 1 — Get today's petrol price for your city.** Our [petrol price by city page](/petrol-price-in-india/) lists current rates for every major Indian city, updated daily. Oil company apps (Fuel@IOC, HP Pay, BPCL Purse) show the same. Don't use another city's price — state VAT makes prices differ by ₹8–15 between states, and that directly moves your per-km number.

**Step 2 — Get a realistic mileage figure.** Not the ARAI number on the brochure. ARAI figures are lab-claimed under artificial conditions; real owners typically report 15–25% lower. The honest sources are owner forums (Team-BHP, model-specific Facebook groups) or — best of all — your own tank-to-tank record from Method 2 below.

**Step 3 — Divide.** Price per litre ÷ real km per litre. That's your cost per km.

Here's what that produces at Delhi's ₹102.12 per litre (12 October 2026) across the vehicles most Indians actually ride and drive:

| Vehicle | Realistic mileage | Cost per km |
|---|---|---|
| 100–110cc commuter bike (Splendor, Platina) | ~45 km/l | **₹2.27** |
| 110cc scooter (Activa, Jupiter) | ~35 km/l | **₹2.92** |
| 125cc bike (Pulsar 125, Shine 125) | ~40 km/l | **₹2.55** |
| 350cc classic bike (Classic 350) | ~20 km/l | **₹5.11** |
| Petrol hatchback, manual (Swift, i20, Baleno city driving) | ~18 km/l | **₹5.67** |
| Petrol hatchback, AMT/CVT | ~15 km/l | **₹6.81** |
| Compact sedan / mid-size petrol sedan (Amaze, City) | ~14–15 km/l | **₹6.81 – ₹7.30** |
| Mid-size petrol SUV (Creta, Seltos petrol) | ~12 km/l | **₹8.51** |
| Large petrol SUV (Fortuner petrol-class) | ~9–11 km/l | **₹9.28 – ₹11.35** |

Two things jump out. First, the spread is enormous — the same ₹102 petrol costs a biker ₹2.27 a kilometre and an SUV owner ₹9–11. That's a four-to-five times difference in the metric nobody measures. Second, small changes in mileage matter more than you think: an AMT hatchback at 15 km/l pays ₹1.14 more per kilometre than the same car's manual at 18 km/l — over 15,000 km a year, that's ₹17,000 for the convenience of not operating a clutch.

Use this method for ballpark decisions. For anything where real money rides on the answer — a new car purchase, a budget that has to hold for a year — use Method 2.

## Method 2: the tank-to-tank test (the accurate one)

The simple formula inherits every error in your assumed mileage. Tank-to-tank removes the guesswork entirely: it measures the mileage your car *actually* returns, on *your* roads, with *your* driving, and converts it to cost per km from the fuel you physically paid for. This is the method I've used for every number I quote on this site.

**Step 1 — Fill the tank completely.** Drive to any pump and fill until the auto-cut clicks. Not "almost full." The method depends on starting from a known full state. Note your odometer reading the moment you finish (or take a photo of the pump screen and the odometer together).

**Step 2 — Drive normally for a week or two.** Don't change your route, don't hypermile, don't avoid the office canteen's parking. The whole point is to measure your *actual* pattern — a mix of city traffic, weekend runs, maybe a highway stretch.

**Step 3 — Fill completely again.** Same pump if possible (same nozzle, same auto-cut behaviour). Note two numbers this time: **how many litres the pump dispensed** and **the new odometer reading**.

**Step 4 — Do the two divisions.**

- Kilometres driven = second odometer reading − first odometer reading
- Mileage = kilometres driven ÷ litres filled
- Cost per km = total money paid at the second fill ÷ kilometres driven

A real example from my own log, hatchback in Delhi:

- Fill A: full tank, odometer at 42,150 km
- Two weeks of normal driving, one weekend trip to Gurugram
- Fill B: 22.8 litres at ₹102.12 = **₹2,328.34**; odometer now 42,562 km
- Kilometres covered: 42,562 − 42,150 = **412 km**
- Mileage: 412 ÷ 22.8 = **18.07 km/l**
- Cost per km: ₹2,328.34 ÷ 412 = **₹5.65**

That ₹5.65 is not an estimate. It's the exact amount of money that left my pocket to move that car 412 kilometres, on my routes, in my traffic. No assumption enters the calculation.

**One or two cycles isn't enough.** A single tank-to-tank cycle is honest but noisy — a heavy-traffic fortnight inflates it, a highway weekend deflates it. Run the test across **6–10 fill-ups** (roughly three months of normal driving) and average the results. That's the number I'd stake money on, and it's the number I'd use for any decision involving more than a month's expense.

## Method 3: the fill-up log (the budgeting method)

Methods 1 and 2 answer "what does a kilometre cost?" Method 3 answers a different, equally important question: "where is my money actually going?"

It's simple: keep a note on your phone — every fill-up gets one line — date, litres, rupees paid, odometer reading. Sixty seconds at the pump, for the rest of your life. At the end of each month, three numbers fall out of it for free:

1. **Total fuel spend** — the sum of rupees paid. This is your actual monthly petrol bill, not an estimate.
2. **Total kilometres driven** — last odometer minus first.
3. **Effective cost per km** — total spend ÷ total km. This is your real, blended rate across city, highway, monsoon crawling and January emptiness.

The blended number is the one that beats Method 1. Over a quarter, it automatically averages out your tank-to-tank cycles *and* weights them by how much you actually spent in each driving mode. When someone asks me "what's your cost per km," the number I quote is the Method 3 average — my current figure sits at ₹5.55–₹5.75 depending on the month's mix, against ₹5.67 from the pure formula at 18 km/l.

## Turning cost per km into a monthly budget

The formula's real power shows up when you stop thinking in litres and start thinking in kilometres — because your life is scheduled in kilometres, not litres.

**Your monthly commute.** Say you drive 12 km each way to work — 24 km a day, 22 working days a month, 528 km. On a petrol hatchback at ₹5.67/km, your office commute alone costs **₹2,993 a month**. Add 200 km of weekend running and you're at ₹4,130. If you're considering a house or a job on the far side of the city, that multiplication is the first thing to do — a 20 km one-way commute instead of 12 adds 352 km a month, or nearly ₹2,000, before you've counted the time.

**Your annual fuel bill.** 15,000 km a year — a realistic average for a city car — at ₹5.67/km is **₹85,050**. Ten thousand kilometres is ₹56,700. Twenty-five thousand is ₹1,41,750. Pick your real annual distance from your last year's odometer readings, multiply by your cost per km, and you have a budget number that actually holds.

**A road trip.** Delhi to Jaipur is about 280 km one way. Round trip plus local running: 660 km × ₹5.67 = **₹3,742** in petrol alone. Add tolls (₹600–900 for that route on a car) and you have the trip's transport budget before hotel and food. The same multiplication works for any route — measure the distance, multiply by your rate.

**Bike vs car vs cab.** This is where cost-per-km thinking gets genuinely decision-shaped. A commuter bike at ₹2.27/km covers 528 km of monthly office commuting for ₹1,199. The same commute in a petrol hatchback costs ₹2,993. A cab at roughly ₹8–12 per kilometre (depending on city, surge, and whether it's a sedan) lands somewhere around ₹4,200–₹6,300 for the same distance. Three transport modes, one month, one commute — the entire cost hierarchy visible in three multiplications. (The full version of that comparison, including diesel and CNG, is in [petrol vs diesel vs CNG running costs](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/).)

## E20, mileage drops, and the hidden rise in your per-km cost

Two things silently move your cost per km upward even when the pump price doesn't change at all.

**E20 petrol.** India's ethanol-blended petrol (E20, 20% ethanol) is now the standard supply across most of the country. Ethanol carries slightly less energy per litre than pure petrol, and while modern flex-fuel-compatible engines compensate with timing and mapping, real-world reports from owners consistently put the mileage impact in the **3–5% range** for older non-optimised engines, near zero for the latest generation. That sounds small until you run it through the formula: a hatchback at 18 km/l dropping to 17.1 km/l (5%) moves from ₹5.67 to **₹5.97 per kilometre** — thirty paisa more, every kilometre, forever. Over 15,000 km a year, that's ₹4,500 you didn't budget for. The full mechanism, which engines are affected, and how to check your car's compatibility is in [what is E20 petrol](/blog/what-is-e20-petrol/).

**Unexplained mileage drops.** If your tank-to-tank mileage has quietly fallen from 18 to 15 km/l, your cost per km has jumped from ₹5.67 to ₹6.81 — an 20% increase in your running cost with zero change in petrol price. When that happens, the cause is usually one of: bad fuel from a pump (yes, it happens — I [tested my own bike's mileage across three pumps](/blog/why-my-motorcycle-mileage-dropped-fuel-pump-tests/) and found a 12% spread), under-inflated tyres, a clogged air filter, stale spark plugs, or driving-pattern changes you haven't noticed. This is exactly why I recommend running the tank-to-tank test every few months even if you're not trying to optimise anything — it catches mechanical and fuel-quality problems that show up in your wallet long before they show up as a breakdown.

## Six decisions this one number actually settles

Cost per km isn't an abstract metric. Here are the real-life questions it answers, all with the same multiplication:

**1. "Can I afford this commute?"** Distance × 22 days × 2 × your cost per km = monthly fuel cost of the job or the house. Compare it to the salary difference or the rent difference you're weighing.

**2. "Petrol car or diesel/CNG?"** Cost per km is the first input — but only the first. The full decision needs the breakeven math (how many kilometres until the higher purchase price of the diesel or CNG variant pays itself back), which is worked out in full in [which car is best, petrol or diesel](/blog/which-car-is-best-petrol-or-diesel/) and the [petrol vs diesel vs CNG comparison](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/).

**3. "Drive my car or take a cab?"** Multiply your commute distance by both rates. For a single person doing a 40 km daily round trip in a city with decent cabs, the two numbers are often closer than expected — and when you add parking, driver fatigue and the car's fixed costs (insurance, service, depreciation — none of which appear in fuel cost per km), the cab sometimes wins on total cost even while losing on fuel.

**4. "Should I take this road trip?"** Route distance × 2 (or your actual itinerary) × cost per km = the fuel line of your trip budget. Add tolls. Compare against the train or flight.

**5. "Is this 'great mileage' claim real?"** When a seller, a friend or a brochure claims 22 km/l for a car you're buying, run it through the formula and sanity-check it against owner-reported figures on forums. 22 km/l at ₹102.12 is ₹4.64/km; 16 km/l is ₹6.38/km — a ₹1.74 per-kilometre difference that compounds to ₹26,000 a year at 15,000 km.

**6. "Bike, scooter, or car for this specific use?"** Short solo city runs are where two-wheelers crush cars on cost per km — ₹2.27–₹2.92 against ₹5.67–₹7.30. Run the multiplication for your real weekly distance before deciding a car is "necessary."

## Common mistakes people make

- **Trusting ARAI mileage for the calculation.** Lab figures are tested under conditions nobody drives in. Owner-reported numbers run 15–25% lower; your own tank-to-tank record is the only truly reliable figure.
- **Using one fill-up as your mileage.** A single tank skews to whatever that fortnight's driving looked like. Six to ten cycles, averaged, is the standard that removes the noise.
- **Using another city's petrol price.** State VAT stacks differently everywhere — ₹102 in Delhi and ₹113 in Jaipur are both real, and using the wrong one misstates your cost by more than 10%. Always use your own city's live rate.
- **Never recalculating.** Petrol prices move every few weeks and your driving pattern drifts over the year. Recompute at least quarterly, or any time the price shifts by ₹3 or more.
- **Mixing fuel cost with total running cost.** Cost per km covers petrol only. Insurance, service, tyres, parking and depreciation are a separate (larger) number — the full breakdown is in the [running costs comparison](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/). Keep the two metrics separate or you'll double-count.
- **Partial fills and misreading the pump.** Tank-to-tank only works from full-to-full. Filling ₹500 "top-ups" makes the arithmetic meaningless — and the odd-litre trick that some pumps use (showing a rounded figure while dispensing slightly less) is worth knowing about when the numbers look suspicious.
- **Ignoring E20 effects.** If you moved to E20 supply in the last two years and never re-measured your mileage, your cost per km is probably not what you think it is.
- **Optimising the wrong lever.** Route tricks and tyre pressures save single-digit percentages. Fuel choice, vehicle choice and mileage reality save double-digit ones — see [why diesel gives more mileage](/blog/why-diesel-gives-more-mileage/) and [why CNG is better than petrol](/blog/why-cng-is-better-than-petrol/) for the levers that actually move ₹50,000+ a year.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the formula to calculate petrol cost per km?

**Cost per km = petrol price per litre ÷ mileage in km per litre.** At Delhi's ₹102.12 per litre and a real-world 18 km/l, that's ₹5.67 per kilometre. Multiply cost per km by any distance — a trip, a month, a year — to get the rupee total.

### How do I calculate cost per km if I don't know my mileage?

Use the tank-to-tank method: fill your tank completely, note the odometer, drive normally for a week or two, fill completely again, and divide the kilometres covered by the litres dispensed at the second fill. Divide the rupees paid at that fill by the kilometres covered and you have cost per km directly — no separate mileage calculation needed.

### What is a "good" cost per km in India?

For a petrol two-wheeler, ₹2.30–₹3.00 per km is normal. For a petrol hatchback, ₹5.50–₹6.00. For a petrol sedan or compact SUV, ₹6.80–₹8.50. Larger petrol SUVs run ₹9–₹11+. Numbers worse than these ranges usually mean either poor mileage or an expensive city — check both.

### Does E20 petrol increase my cost per km?

It can, slightly. Ethanol's lower energy density commonly shows up as a 3–5% mileage drop on engines not specifically optimised for E20 — which at 18 km/l and ₹102/l works out to ₹0.20–₹0.30 more per kilometre, or roughly ₹3,000–₹4,500 a year at 15,000 km. Latest-generation engines largely eliminate the difference. Details in [what is E20 petrol](/blog/what-is-e20-petrol/).

### How do I calculate my monthly petrol budget from cost per km?

Multiply your cost per km by the kilometres you expect to drive that month. If you drive 12 km each way to work, 22 days a month, plus about 200 km of other running: (12 × 2 × 22) + 200 = 728 km. At ₹5.67/km that's ₹4,128. The [fuel cost calculator](/calculator) does this for any distance you enter.

### Is it cheaper to drive a petrol car or take a cab?

Depends entirely on distance and occupancy. For a 40 km daily solo commute, a hatchback at ₹5.67/km costs about ₹2,500 for 440 km of office travel, while a cab at ₹8–12/km runs ₹3,500–₹5,300 for the same distance — but the car's fixed costs (insurance, service, depreciation, parking) can easily add ₹4,000–₹6,000 a month that fuel cost per km doesn't include. For low-distance or occasional use, the cab usually wins on total cost. For high-distance family use, the car usually wins.

### How often should I recalculate my cost per km?

Once a quarter at minimum, or whenever your city's petrol price moves by ₹3 or more. If you've switched to E20 supply, changed tyres, or noticed a behaviour change in the car, recalculate immediately — those silently alter the mileage input.

### Can I use this method for diesel and CNG?

Yes — same formula, different units. Diesel: price per litre ÷ km per litre. CNG: price per kilogram ÷ km per kg (CNG mileage is measured per kilogram, not per litre, which is why direct price comparisons at the pump are meaningless). At today's Delhi rates, diesel hatchbacks land around ₹4.30/km and CNG around ₹3.60–₹3.95/km against petrol's ₹5.67 — the full three-fuel comparison with breakeven math is in [petrol vs diesel vs CNG running costs](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/).

### Does cost per km include maintenance and insurance?

No — it's petrol cost only, which is what makes it a clean, comparable metric. Total running cost per km adds insurance, service, tyres, parking and depreciation, and is typically 1.5–2× the fuel-only figure for a car. Use cost per km for fuel decisions (commute budgets, trip planning, fuel-type comparisons); use total running cost for ownership decisions (buy petrol vs diesel vs CNG, keep vs sell).
