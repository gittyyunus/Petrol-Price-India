The question itself looks simple. "How much petrol for 500 km?" But ask five owners the same question and you'll get five different answers — 20 litres, 25 litres, 30 litres, "arre 500 km toh ek tank mein ho jaata hai" — and every one of them is right *for their car*. That's the trap. Fuel required for a fixed distance isn't a property of petrol. It's a property of your car's mileage, your city's price, and the roads you drive — and those three inputs move the answer by a factor of three.

So this article works the number out properly: the one formula behind it, the exact litres and cost for every common Indian car category at today's prices, whether a single tank physically covers 500 km, what the same distance costs in different cities, and how city driving versus highway driving quietly changes the answer by 10 litres for an identical odometer reading.

## The formula: two divisions, that's all

Everything here rests on two lines of arithmetic:

**Litres required = Distance ÷ Mileage (km per litre)**

**Cost = Litres required × Petrol price per litre**

That's it. If your car returns 18 km/l in real driving and petrol in your city costs ₹102.12 a litre (Delhi's rate on 12 October 2026), then 500 km needs 500 ÷ 18 = **27.8 litres**, which costs 27.8 × 102.12 = **₹2,837**.

The formula is trivial. The two things people get wrong are the inputs — an inflated mileage figure from the brochure instead of the road, and another city's petrol price instead of their own. Both errors are fixable, and both are worth fixing, because together they can misstate the answer by 30% or more.

Three things are worth noticing in that table.

**The spread is enormous.** The same 500 km, on the same petrol, on the same day — anywhere between 20 litres and 62 litres depending purely on the car. "How much petrol for 500 km" has no single answer, and any article or app that gives you one without asking about your car is guessing.

**Mileage swamps everything else.** Moving from 15 km/l to 18 km/l — the difference between a CVT hatch in traffic and the same car on a free-flowing road — saves 5.5 litres, or about ₹570 per 500 km. That's more than the price gap between the cheapest and most expensive cities for the same distance.

**The hatchback number is the one most Indians need.** A real-world 18 km/l petrol hatchback is the country's most common car profile, and it's the anchor for every number in this article: **about 28 litres and ₹2,840 for 500 km.** If you drive one, you can stop reading here — unless you also want to know whether that fits in one tank, which is next.

## Will one tank cover 500 km? The capacity check

The litres table answers "how much petrol." The follow-up question every trip-planner asks is "do I need to fill up *en route*?" — and that depends on your tank, not just your mileage.

Rule of thumb for Indian cars: **hatchbacks carry roughly 35–42 litres, sedans 40–45, compact SUVs 45–50, and large SUVs 70–80.** Multiply your tank capacity by your real mileage and you have your theoretical range — then subtract about 10% for the reserve you should never burn, because running a tank dry damages the fuel pump, which costs far more than the petrol you'd save.

Run the check for the common cases:

- **Hatchback at 18 km/l with a 37-litre tank:** 37 × 18 = 666 km theoretical, ~600 km usable. **500 km fits — on one tank, with margin.**
- **Compact SUV at 12 km/l with a 50-litre tank:** 50 × 12 = 600 km theoretical, ~540 km usable. It fits, but comfortably refuelling before you start is cheaper than betting on reserve light behaviour on a highway.
- **Large SUV at 9 km/l with an 80-litre tank:** 720 km theoretical. One tank, easily.
- **The awkward case — AMT hatch at 15 km/l with a 32-litre tank:** 480 km theoretical, 430 km usable. **500 km does *not* fit.** Plan one fill-up en route.

That last row is the useful one. The cars with the smallest tanks and the weakest real-world mileage are exactly the ones that can't do 500 km on a single fill — and they're often driven by the people doing their first long road trip. Check the arithmetic *before* you leave, not at the reserve light near the Rajasthan border.

## 500 km of city vs 500 km of highway: the hidden 10 litres

Here's the part most fuel articles skip. "500 km" is not one quantity — the *same* 500 km burns very different amounts of petrol depending on where those kilometres happen.

- **500 km of highway at 22 km/l:** 22.7 litres — **₹2,321**
- **500 km of mixed driving at 18 km/l:** 27.8 litres — **₹2,837**
- **500 km of stop-start city traffic at 14 km/l:** 35.7 litres — **₹3,647**

Same distance, same car, same fuel price — and a ₹1,300 spread between the highway and city extremes, purely because idling, braking and re-accelerating convert petrol into heat and noise instead of distance. This matters practically in two places: when you're budgeting a trip (a route through two city centres can cost you hundreds more than the same distance on an open road), and when someone quotes you a "500 km" figure from their driving — always ask *which* 500 km.

A related trap: the mileage you get *on the trip* will likely be better than your car's usual figure, because highway running is genuinely more efficient. If your car does 15 km/l in daily traffic, a 500 km highway run might return 18–19. The petrol needed drops to about 27 litres. Don't budget with the city number for a highway trip — and don't budget with the highway number for a month of office runs.

## Planning an actual 500 km trip: the full budget

Let's make it concrete. A 500 km weekend is a real itinerary for most Indian families — Delhi to Agra and back with local running, a Bengaluru–Mysuru–Coorg loop, Mumbai to Nashik and around. Here's the transport budget for the standard 18 km/l hatchback, Delhi rates:

- **Petrol:** 27.8 litres × ₹102.12 = **₹2,837**
- **Tolls:** ₹500–900 depending on route (the Delhi–Agra expressway alone is ₹485 for a car)
- **Realistic total:** **₹3,300–₹3,700** for fuel and tolls

## What silently changes the number

The formula is stable; the inputs drift. These are the factors that move your 500 km litre-count without you noticing:

**E20 petrol.** India's standard supply is now 20% ethanol blend, and on engines not fully optimised for it, real-world mileage commonly drops 3–5%. On our 18 km/l hatchback that's 18 → 17.1 km/l — 29.2 litres instead of 27.8 for the same 500 km, or about **₹147 more per trip, every trip**. Check your car's E20 compatibility in [what is E20 petrol](/blog/what-is-e20-petrol/).

**A quiet mileage drop.** If your real mileage has fallen from 18 to 15 km/l — bad fuel, under-inflated tyres, a clogged air filter, stale plugs — 500 km now needs 33.3 litres instead of 27.8. That's ₹570 more per 500 km, and at 15,000 km a year, over ₹17,000. I once [tested my own bike's mileage across three different pumps](/blog/why-my-motorcycle-mileage-dropped-fuel-pump-tests/) and found a 12% spread from fuel quality alone — the cause is often mundane, but the cost is not. The full checklist of what to check, and what each fix costs, is in [how I cut my fuel bill by 15%](/blog/how-i-cut-my-fuel-bill-by-15-percent/).

**Air conditioning.** Negligible on the highway (a few percent at steady speed), but 5–10% in stop-start city driving, where the compressor loads the engine exactly when you're already idling. On a 500 km city-heavy run at 14 km/l, AC can add 2–3 litres.

**Speed.** Aerodynamic drag rises with the square of speed. A car that returns 18 km/l at 80–90 km/h often returns 14–15 at 120. If your 500 km is a highway run at touring speed, budget toward the higher litre figure — the speed-vs-mileage trade is the biggest discretionary lever you have on a trip.

**Boot weight and roof carriers.** A fully loaded boot plus a roof box can cost 5–10% of highway mileage. If you're packing for five people for a week, that's a litre or two per 100 km nobody budgets for.

**Tyre pressure.** 10–15% below spec costs roughly 2–3% of your mileage. Check it cold, before the trip, with the boot loaded as it will be.

## Diesel or CNG for the same 500 km?

If you're doing lots of 500 km runs, the fuel-type question is worth one honest pass. At Delhi rates (petrol ₹102.12/l, diesel ₹95.20/l, CNG ₹86.98/kg):

| Fuel | Typical real mileage | Fuel for 500 km | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petrol (hatchback) | 18 km/l | 27.8 l | **₹2,837** |
| Diesel (hatchback) | 22 km/l | 22.7 l | ₹2,163 |
| CNG (factory-fitted) | 24 km/kg | 20.8 kg | ₹1,813 |

Diesel saves about ₹675 per 500 km on a hatchback; CNG saves over ₹1,000. Those are real numbers — but they're only half the decision. A diesel or CNG variant costs ₹1–2 lakh more to buy, which needs tens of thousands of kilometres to pay back, and CNG cars spend part of every trip running on petrol while the switchover completes. The breakeven math — and exactly when each fuel wins — is in [which car is best, petrol or diesel](/blog/which-car-is-best-petrol-or-diesel/) and [why CNG is cheaper per kilometre than petrol](/blog/why-cng-is-better-than-petrol/). Short version: if you're covering 25,000+ km a year, the alternative-fuel maths deserves an evening; if you're doing a few 500 km trips a month in a car you already own, petrol is simply the number you budget.

## Common mistakes

- **Using ARAI mileage.** Brochure figures are lab numbers under conditions nobody drives in; owner-reported real-world mileage runs 15–25% lower. Enter the real number or you'll under-budget the trip by several litres.
- **Ignoring the reserve.** 27.8 litres is the *theoretical* requirement. Fill 30 — the ₹200 extra buys you the freedom to skip an overpriced highway pump and protects the fuel pump.
- **Budgeting with another city's price.** A ₹10/litre difference between cities is ₹280 on a 28-litre requirement. Always use your own city's rate.
- **Assuming 500 km = one tank without checking.** Multiply your tank capacity by your real mileage first. Small hatchbacks with weak mileage genuinely can't make it.
- **Treating one trip's good mileage as the car's mileage.** A highway run at 22 km/l will tempt you to believe it forever. It won't hold in traffic. Measure across several fills.
- **Forgetting the fuel-type cost isn't the total cost.** Diesel and CNG are cheaper per kilometre and dearer to buy. Fuel math alone never settles the purchase decision.
- **Not recalculating after a price change.** Rates move; so does your driving. Recheck at least quarterly — the calculator takes the same two minutes as last time.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much petrol is required for 500 km in a car?

**Typically 20 to 35 litres, depending on your car's mileage.** A fuel-efficient hatchback at 22–25 km/l needs 20–23 litres (₹2,050–₹2,320). The most common Indian car profile — a petrol hatchback at 18 km/l — needs **27.8 litres, about ₹2,840** at Delhi prices. A compact SUV at 12 km/l needs 41.7 litres (₹4,255). Enter your own mileage and city in the [fuel calculator](/calculator) for your exact figure.

### How much does 500 km cost in petrol?

For the standard 18 km/l petrol hatchback at ₹102.12 per litre: **₹2,837 in petrol alone**, plus tolls if it's a highway trip. City-wise, the same 500 km ranges from about ₹2,800 in Chennai to ₹3,140 in Jaipur. Add ₹500–900 of tolls for a typical intercity route.

### Can my car do 500 km on one full tank?

Multiply your tank capacity by your real mileage. A 37-litre hatchback at 18 km/l covers ~600 km usable (after reserving ~10%) — 500 km fits comfortably. A small-tank, low-mileage combination (say 32 litres at 15 km/l = ~430 km usable) does not, and needs one fill-up en route. Check before you leave; don't rely on the reserve light.

### How much petrol is needed for 500 km on a bike or scooter?

A commuter bike at 45 km/l needs 11.1 litres (₹1,135). A 110cc scooter at 35 km/l needs 14.3 litres (₹1,460). A 350cc classic at 20 km/l needs 25 litres (₹2,553) — close to a hatchback's requirement, which surprises many riders. Full two-wheeler numbers are in the [cost per km guide](/blog/how-to-calculate-petrol-cost-per-km/).

### Is the 500 km figure the same on the highway and in the city?

No — and the gap is large. 500 km of highway at 22 km/l needs 22.7 litres (₹2,321); 500 km of city traffic at 14 km/l needs 35.7 litres (₹3,647). The same distance, a ₹1,300 difference, because city driving wastes fuel on idling and braking. Always budget by your *route's* expected mileage, not the car's best-case figure.

### Does E20 petrol change how much I need for 500 km?

Slightly, yes. On engines not fully optimised for the 20% ethanol blend, mileage commonly drops 3–5% — our 18 km/l hatchback drops to ~17.1 km/l, taking the 500 km requirement from 27.8 to 29.2 litres, about ₹147 more per trip. Latest-generation engines largely avoid the penalty. Details in [what is E20 petrol](/blog/what-is-e20-petrol/).

### Is diesel or CNG cheaper for 500 km?

Yes, per trip: a diesel hatchback (22 km/l at ₹95.20) needs 22.7 litres — ₹2,163, saving about ₹675. A factory CNG hatchback (24 km/kg at ₹86.98) needs 20.8 kg — ₹1,813, saving over ₹1,000. But both cost more to buy, so the annual distance you drive decides whether the saving pays back. Full breakeven math in the [three-fuel comparison](/blog/petrol-vs-diesel-vs-cng-running-costs/).

### Should I fill up fully before a 500 km trip?

Yes. Start full, at your own city's price — it's usually cheaper than the pumps on the route out, and your litre estimate assumes a known full tank. Carry about 10% reserve margin above the calculated requirement; running a tank dry risks fuel-pump damage that costs more than any petrol you'd save.

### Does 500 km of AC use more petrol?

On the highway, only a few percent — the compressor's load is small at steady speed. In stop-start city driving it's 5–10%, which on a 500 km city-heavy run at 14 km/l adds 2–3 litres, or roughly ₹250. Windows down at speed costs more than AC (drag), so on a mixed trip the AC penalty is smaller than most people assume.

## Final thoughts

"How much petrol for 500 km?" turns out to be a question about *your car*, not about petrol — anywhere from 20 litres in an efficient hatchback to 62 in a big SUV, and the honest middle answer for most Indian drivers is about **28 litres, or ₹2,840 at today's Delhi rate**. The same distance costs ₹300 more in Jaipur than Delhi, ₹1,300 more in traffic than on the highway, and ₹150 more on E20-affected engines. None of those differences are visible on the pump board, and all of them are invisible in a brochure's km/l figure.
